## How the numbers were produced (reproducible)

All scripts are in `audit/scripts/p08/`.
- `sieve.c` / `sieve2.c` is a C segmented sieve run up to p_{10^9} = 22,801,763,489. It reproduces the standard checkpoints exactly:
  - π(10^6..10^10) = 78498 / 664579 / 5761455 / 50847534 / 455052511
  - p_{10^6}, p_{10^7}, p_{10^8}, p_{10^9} = 15485863 / 179424673 / 2038074743 / 22801763489
  - π₂(10^8) = 440,312
- The run covers the first 10^9 primes and records:
  - consecutive-prime transition counts mod 10, mod 3, mod 4 and mod 5
  - the gap histogram
  - a joint (last digit, gap) histogram for the first 10^8 primes
  - π(x;3,1) and π(x;3,2)
- Outputs are in `out_1e8.txt` and `out_1e9.txt`. `analyze.py` turns them into percentages.
- `model.py` is a crude Hardy–Littlewood/Cramér model: P(d1→d2) ∝ Σ_{h≡d2−d1 (10)} S′(h)·e^{−h/log x}. It is run three ways: with the correct p=3 Euler factor, with the paper's claimed p=3 factor, and with no p=3 factor.
- My first-10^8-primes row-1 counts (4,623,041 / 7,429,438 / 7,504,612 / 5,442,344) agree to within ±1 with the LO–S published Table (4,623,042 / 7,429,438 / 7,504,612 / 5,442,345). The ±1 is an endpoint convention.

## CRITICAL

### C1. The core mechanism is backwards: the p = 3 Euler factor does not vanish for gaps ≡ 0 (mod 3); it is at its maximum there. (VERIFIED)

**Where:**
- l.399–410: "so $\mathfrak{S}_6 = 0$ for the ternary factor at $p=3$. The probability mass that would have gone to gap-6 transitions is redistributed"
- l.424–426, Prop 4.1(c): "the $p=3$ Euler factor of the Hardy--Littlewood singular series vanishes for Forbidden-class gaps"
- l.464–474 and l.632–633: "connects directly to the vanishing and non-vanishing of the $p=3$ Euler factor"

**What is wrong.** For the pair {0, h}, the Hardy–Littlewood local factor at p is (1 − ν_p(h)/p)(1 − 1/p)^{−2}.
- When 3 | h: ν_3 = 1, so the factor is (2/3)/(4/9) = **3/2**.
- When 3 ∤ h: ν_3 = 2, so the factor is (1/3)/(4/9) = **3/4**.

So S(h) = 2C₂ ∏_{p|h, p>2} (p−1)/(p−2), and gaps divisible by 3 are favoured by a factor of 2. A local factor vanishes only for inadmissible tuples (e.g. {0,2,4}), never for a pair.

**Evidence.**
- Gap 6 is the most frequent gap at every cut-off: 11.60 M times vs 6.50 M for gap 2 (first 10^8 primes), a ratio of 1.79×; the ratio is 1.81× at 10^9.
- The gap-6 transitions 1→7 (30.02 %) and 3→9 (30.01 %) are among the most frequent of all 16.
- 1→7 is the single most likely successor of a prime ending in 1. It beats the "enhanced" gap-2 transition 1→3 (29.72 %; at 10^9: 29.43 vs 29.30).
- In the crude HL model (`model.py`), the correct p=3 factor reproduces the observed matrix to an RMS error of **0.85 pp**.
- The paper's factor (0 when 3|h) gives an RMS error of **5.9 pp**. It predicts 1→7 = 19.4 %, against 30.0 % observed. It also predicts 1→1 = 24.7 %, i.e. it **destroys the diagonal suppression** the paper sets out to explain.

**Correction.** The ternary contribution to the LO–S bias is that gaps ≡ 0 (mod 3) are *doubled* in the singular series. This boosts 1→7, 3→9 and 7→3. Delete the "vanishing / redistribution" mechanism.

### C2. False singular-series values are given as the cause of the diagonal suppression. (VERIFIED)

**Where:**
- l.380–382: "$\mathfrak{S}_2 = 2\prod_{p>2}\frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2} \approx 1.3203 > 1$, which is the largest singular series value among even gaps"
- l.392: "The Hardy--Littlewood singular series for gap~10 satisfies $\mathfrak{S}_{10} < 1$"
- l.395–396 and Prop 4.1(b), l.422–423: "penalised by the smallest singular series"
- Conclusion (i), l.619–621

**What is wrong.** S(2) = 2C₂ ≈ 1.3203 is the **minimum** over even h. It is tied with every power of 2 (S(4) = S(8) = 1.3203). Other values: S(6) = S(12) = 2.6406, S(10) = 2C₂·4/3 = **1.7604 > 1**, S(30) = 3.5209.
- In base 10, every same-digit gap is a multiple of 10, so it gets the extra p = 5 factor 4/3. The singular series therefore *favours* the diagonal.
- Mod 3 (base 3 or 6), same-class gaps are multiples of 6 and get the maximal p = 3 factor 3/2. They are still suppressed: P(1→1 mod 3) = 44.67 % (first 10^8 primes) and 45.15 % (first 10^9), against 50 %.

The suppression is a gap-length effect: the next prime is unlikely to be far away. In LO–S this is the first-order term c₁·loglog x/log x, with c₁(q;a,a) = 1/2 − φ(q)/2. In `model.py`, deleting all ternary information (setting the p=3 factor to 1) still gives diagonal entries of 19.5–21 %.

**Correction.** State that S(2) is the smallest even-gap singular series and that S(10) = 1.76. Attribute the diagonal suppression to gap length, as LO–S do.

### C4. "Snail's pace = Siegel–Walfisz rate of ternary equilibration" is false. (VERIFIED)

**Where:**
- Abstract l.116–118
- Main result l.164–166
- §5 l.485–510 (eq. (1), Prop 5.1)
- Conclusion (ii) l.622–624
- Fig. 3
- Lean axiom `snails_pace_is_siegel_walfisz`

**What is wrong.**
- (a) The LO–S bias concerns *pairs* of consecutive primes. It exists even with perfect equidistribution of single primes. The LO–S abstract says so explicitly: "the sequence of primes is very well distributed in the reduced residue classes … the distribution of pairs … is surprisingly erratic". A bound on |π₁ − π₂| therefore cannot be its rate.
- (b) LO–S derive the rate explicitly: π(x;q,(a,b)) = li(x)/φ(q)² · (1 + c₁·loglog x/log x + c₂/log x + O((log x)^{−7/4})), with c₁ = 1/2 − φ(q)/2 on the diagonal and 1/2 off it.
- (c) x·exp(−c√log x) is an *upper bound*, not the true size. Under GRH, and empirically, |π₁ − π₂| ≈ √x/log x (Chebyshev bias), which is a polynomial rate.
- (d) exp(−c√log N) decays faster than any power of 1/log N, so it decays faster than loglog N/log N. It cannot be "the snail's pace".

**Evidence.**
- Recomputed π(x;3,2) − π(x;3,1) = 190, 420, 2107, 6264 at x = 10^7..10^10 (√x/log x = 196, 543, 1526, 4343).
- The relative mod-3 imbalance is 4.1·10^−5 at 10^9 and 1.4·10^−5 at 10^10. The digit bias at the same scale is 6.9 pp out of 25 (27 %), more than 3 orders of magnitude larger.
- Between 10^7 and 10^10 the mod-3 relative imbalance falls **20.7×**. The mean diagonal deficit falls only **1.21×** (7.63 pp at p_n = 1.8·10^8 to 6.28 pp at 2.3·10^10). The digit bias does not track ternary equilibration.
- "At exactly the rate specified by (eq:sw)" (l.500) is meaningless for an O-bound.

**Correction.** Delete Prop 5.1 and Fig. 3. Cite the LO–S loglog x/log x term.

### C5. The ternary class of the minimum gap does not determine the effect. The claimed "correct coordinate system" is refuted by the data. (VERIFIED)

**Where:**
- Correction box l.318–324: "The ternary class of this minimum gap governs whether the transition is enhanced, suppressed, or redistributed"
- Table 2 caption l.335–337
- Fig. 2 and Fig. 4 captions

**Evidence.** Average transition frequency by minimum gap (first 10^8 primes):

| Min gap | Class | Average |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | II | 30.47 % |
| 4 | I | 26.39 % |
| 6 | "Forbidden" | 29.02 % |
| 8 | II | 23.29 % |
| 10 | I | 18.13 % |

- The same Class II contains gap 2 (strongly enhanced) and gap 8, all three of whose transitions are *below* 25 % (21.77, 24.04, 24.05).
- The same Class I contains gap 4 (above 25 %) and gap 10 (the most suppressed).
- The data are monotone in gap *size*, with gap 6 boosted by the p=3 factor 3/2. That is exactly what HL predicts and the opposite of the paper's rule.

**Correction.** Withdraw the "min-gap ternary class" mechanism.

## MAJOR

### M1. LO–S are misrepresented, and so is the history. (VERIFIED)

**Where:**
- l.108–111: "could not identify the underlying mechanistic cause: why the structure exists, why it holds across all bases, and why the diminishment rate is precisely what it is"
- l.135–136: "and yet no one had checked"
- l.148–156 and l.233–239: "descriptive"
- l.612: "It has resisted mechanistic explanation for nine years"

**Facts.**
- The LO–S conjecture is formulated for every modulus q, which answers "why all bases". Its first-order term c₁ depends only on φ(q) and on whether a = b.
- It gives the decay rate loglog x/log x explicitly.
- Science News (2016) reports that the pattern "had been noted previously by two separate teams", and that LO–S were the first to give a mathematical explanation.
- LO–S's argument is itself a heuristic built on HL with uniformity assumptions. "If HL holds then the bias follows" (l.150) overstates how rigorous it is, but the paper's alternative adds no proof either.

**Correction.** Describe LO–S accurately: an HL-based heuristic with explicit second-order terms that fits the data very well, for every q.

### M2. "Base-10 projection of the mod-3 constraint" is a category error, and the base-independence argument fails. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.112–115, l.161–163, §6.1 l.533–540, Fig. 4 l.545–553, Conclusion (iii) l.625–626.

**What is wrong.**
- Since gcd(10,3) = 1, CRT makes the last digit of a prime independent of p mod 3. For odd p, the last digit is just p mod 5. The terminal digit carries no ternary information, so there is no "projection".
- Fig. 4 counts "Forbidden-class prime terminal digits (3 and 9)". That contradicts Def 3.1 (13 and 19 are not ≡ 0 mod 3) and the "corrected" mechanism itself.
- In base 6 (equivalently mod 3), the diagonal minimum gap is 6, which is "Forbidden" in the paper's terms. Under C1 this would mean "redistribution". The diagonal is nonetheless suppressed (45.15 %).
- Mod 4, where no ternary digit structure exists, shows almost the same suppression (45.48 %) as mod 3 (45.15 %). This is exactly what the LO–S c₁ = 1/2 − φ(q)/2 predicts, since φ = 2 for both.

**Correction.** Remove the base-independence claims, or replace them with the LO–S general-q statement.

### M3. Wrong statement about which diagonal entries are most suppressed. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.208–210: "most pronounced for primes ending in~1 (suppressed to~18\%) and~9 (suppressed to~17\%) and mildest for primes ending in~7 (suppressed to~19\%)".

**Data.** At 10^8 primes, 3→3 = 17.77 % and 7→7 = 17.76 % are the *most* suppressed, and 1→1 = 9→9 = 18.49 % the *least*. At 10^9 the values are 18.45 / 18.44 against 19.00 / 19.00. By the (a,b) ↔ (−b,−a) symmetry, 1→1 equals 9→9 and 3→3 equals 7→7, so 17 % vs 18 % is impossible.

**Correction.** "Strongest for 3 and 7 (≈17.8 %), weakest for 1 and 9 (≈18.5 %), first 10^8 primes."

### M4. The 65 % headline is internally contradicted and misread. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.143–144 says "roughly 65\% more often than by another prime ending in~9". This is correct: (9→1)/(9→9) = 1.648 for the first 10^9 primes, and 1.729 for 10^8.

**The contradictions.**
- The paper's own table (l.202) gives 41/17 = 2.41, i.e. 141 % more.
- Table 2 (l.344) writes "${\sim}41\%$, $1.64\times$". The "65 % more than 9→9" was misread as "1.64 × the 25 % baseline". That misreading is evidently the source of the bogus 41 %. The actual enhancement of 9→1 over 25 % is 1.28× (10^8 primes).

**Correction.** Use 31.97 % (1.28×).

### M5. The twin-prime count is wrong. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.289–290: "verified computationally for all 3{,}424{,}009 twin prime pairs with $p \leq 10^9$".

**Data.** π₂(10^9) = **3,424,506** (recomputed; standard value), or 3,424,505 excluding (3,5). Also, (3,5) is not an "exception": it is excluded by the hypothesis p > 3. A computational "verification" of a two-line congruence argument carries no evidential weight.

**Correction.** Use 3,424,506, or drop the remark.

### M6. The §4.4 formula for the p=3 factor is inverted and mis-evaluated. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.459–469: "$\mathfrak{S}_3(k) = \frac{(1-1/3)^{-1}(1-\chi_3(k)/\phi(3))}{1} = \ldots \tfrac{1}{2}$ if $k \not\equiv 0$".

**What is wrong.**
- χ₃(k) counts the admissible starting classes, which is a favourable count. Using 1 − χ/2 inverts the meaning.
- The formula gives 0 when 3|k, where the true value is 3/2.
- Even on its own terms, the formula evaluates to (3/2)(1 − 1/2) = **3/4**, not the stated 1/2, when 3∤k.
- The normalisation for pairs should be (1 − 1/3)^{−2}, not ^{−1}.
- "admissible $k$-tuple with common difference $k$" conflates tuple size with gap.

**Correction.** S₃(h) = (χ₃(h)/2)/(2/3) = 3/2 if 3|h, and 3/4 otherwise.

### M7. The Fig. 3 caption makes a false asymptotic comparison and claims "confirmation" with no data. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.516–523: "The Siegel--Walfisz envelope $e^{-c\sqrt{\log N}}$ decays far more slowly than any polynomial rate … or logarithmic rate ($1/\log N$)" and "the bias persists into astronomical and trans-computational scales, confirming…".

**What is wrong.**
- e^{−c√L} = o(L^{−A}) for every A, so it decays *faster* than 1/log N.
- For c = 1, e^{−√L} < 1/L for every L > 0, since √L − ln L ≥ 2 − ln 4 > 0.
- The right panel plots a hypothetical curve (with an unspecified c) out to N = 10^47. It cannot confirm anything.

**Correction.** Delete the figure.

### M8. "Gap-2 transitions carry the twin-prime signature" is false for most of the transitions so labelled. (VERIFIED)

**Where:**
- l.376–386: "These transitions are constitutionally enhanced: they represent pairs whose structure is closest to twin prime pairs. The $9\to1$ transition is most enhanced … because its gap-2 landing is arithmetically most accessible"
- Fig. 2 caption

**Data (joint digit/gap histogram, first 10^8 primes).**
- Only **27.1 %** of 9→1 transitions have gap 2; **38.1 %** have gap 12, which is ≡ 0 mod 3 and so in the paper's "Forbidden" class.
- 1→3: 29.1 % have gap 2 and 37.6 % gap 12. 7→9: 29.2 % and 37.5 %.

- 9→1 exceeds 1→3 because of within-row competition: from digit 1 the gap-6 competitor 1→7 takes mass, while from 9 the competitor 9→3 has gap 4. In `model.py`, removing the p=3 factor reverses the order (29.7 vs 30.7). That is the opposite of the paper's mechanism.

### M9. The parity barrier is misdescribed and mis-cited. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.559–562: "The Selberg parity barrier~\cite{Sel50} states that sieve methods operating modulo~2 cannot distinguish … This structural ceiling stalled the bound on prime gaps at~246 (Maynard~\cite{May15}, Polymath8b)".

**What is wrong.**
- The parity problem concerns sieve weights being unable to detect the parity of Ω(n) (the Liouville function). It has nothing to do with "operating modulo 2".
- [Sel50], "An elementary proof of the PNT for arithmetic progressions" (Canad. J. Math. 1950), is not the parity-problem source. That is Selberg, "On elementary methods in prime number theory and their limitations", Trondheim 1949, or his sieve lectures.
- 246 is Polymath8b's bound. Maynard's paper gives 600.
- 246 is set by the Bombieri–Vinogradov level 1/2. Parity is what blocks going below 6 even under GEH.
- Polymath8b has no bibliography entry.

### M10. The parity-section gap table and its conclusions are false. (VERIFIED)

**Where:** l.567–585: "4 & 2 & 0 (Forbidden) & $\times$ Hits Forbidden class" and "The gap-2 ternary signature $(2,1)$ is the only even-gap signature that avoids the Forbidden class for both members. Gap~4 immediately hits the Forbidden class."

- "modulo~2, all even gaps are identical" has nothing to do with the parity barrier.
