CONDITION W3: ABSENCE OF SIEGEL ZEROS FOR CHARACTERS MODULO \(3^K\)

KHAYYAM WAKIL

Abstract. We prove Condition W3: for every primitive Dirichlet character \(\chi \) modulo \(q = 3^K\), the \(L\)-function \(L(s, \chi )\) has no real zero in the region \(\sigma > 1 - c/\!\log q\), for an absolute, eﬀectively computable constant \(c > 0\). The statement is classical, and its proof takes one line: the group \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) is cyclic of even order, so the only real non-principal character modulo \(3^K\) is the one induced from the character \(\chi _{-3}\) modulo \(3\); an exceptional zero can only belong to a real character; and \(L(\sigma ,\chi _{-3}) > 0\) for \(\sigma > 0\). As context we record, with corrections, the arithmetic of \(\Q (\omega )\) that the original version used: the base change \(\chi \circ \mathrm {N}\) of a character \(\chi \) modulo \(3^K\) takes the value \(\chi (n)^2\) on the ideal \((n)\), its conductor is \(\mathfrak p^{2K-1}\) for \(K \geq 2\), and \(L(s,\chi \circ \mathrm {N}) = L(s,\chi )L(s,\chi \chi _{-3})\). Condition W3 does not make any level of distribution unconditional, and nothing in it is speciﬁc to the prime \(3\).

Date: February 2026; revised November 2026.

2020 Mathematics Subject Classiﬁcation. 11M20, 11R42, 11R11 .

Key words and phrases. Siegel zeros, exceptional zeros, Dirichlet \(L\)-functions, cyclic groups, Hecke characters, Eisenstein integers, cascade moduli.

Revision note (November 2026). The proof through a “Stark–Baker theorem” for CM ﬁelds is replaced by the one-line cyclicity argument, and the former Theorem 3.2 is withdrawn: as stated, it would solve the Landau–Siegel zero problem. The lifting identity \(\chi (n) = \Psi _\chi ((n))\) of the former Proposition 2.5 is withdrawn: the base change \(\chi \circ \Nf \) gives \(\chi (n)^2\), its conductor is \(\pp ^{2K-1}\) (not \(\pp ^{2K}\)) for \(K \geq 2\), and the norm map onto \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) has image of index \(2\). The claim that the argument is speciﬁc to \(3\) (former Proposition 5.1) is withdrawn, as is the statement that \(2\) splits in \(\Zomega \) (it is inert, since \(-3 \equiv 5 \pmod 8\)). The table of “Watt conditions” (former Theorem 7.1), the claim that W3 implies \(\theta _f \leq 7/32\), the zero-density estimate quoted with it, and the “unconditional level of distribution \(5/8\)” (former Corollary 7.2) are withdrawn. The case \(K = 1\), the Minkowski bound and the computation of \(\Nf (1-\omega )\) are corrected, and the reference to a “[NOTE]” in the companion paper, which does not exist there, is removed.

1. Setup and Statement

1.1. Context. This paper is p07 of a nine-paper programme [9]. The companion paper [10] (p04) concerns primes in arithmetic progressions to the moduli \(q = 3^K\). The original version of the present paper presented Condition W3 below as the third of three hypotheses “(W1)–(W3)” of Watt’s theorem [8], and claimed that proving it made a level of distribution \(5/8\) for these moduli unconditional. Both claims are withdrawn (Section 6): Watt’s theorem has no such hypotheses, and the level \(5/8\) for each single modulus \(3^K\) is a conjecture in [10], which would go beyond what the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis gives. The name “Condition W3” is kept as a label for the following classical statement.

1.2. Main theorem.

Theorem 1.1 (Condition W3). Let \(K \geq 1\) and let \(\chi \) be a primitive Dirichlet character modulo \(q = 3^K\). Then \(L(s,\chi )\) has no real zero in the region \[ \sigma > 1 - \frac {c}{\log q} \] for an absolute, eﬀectively computable constant \(c > 0\).

The proof (Section 2) uses only the cyclicity of \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \), the classical zero-free region for Dirichlet \(L\)-functions, and the positivity of \(L(\sigma ,\chi _{-3})\). Sections 3 and 4 record, with corrections, the arithmetic of \(\Qomega \) that the original version used; it is context only and is not needed for the proof. Section 5 explains why the “Stark–Baker theorem” of the original version is withdrawn, and Section 6 explains why W3 has no consequence for any level of distribution beyond the Siegel–Walﬁsz range.

1.3. Notation and conventions. Throughout: \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/3}\), so \(\omega ^2 + \omega + 1 = 0\). We write \(\K = \Qomega = \Q (\sqrt {-3})\), with ring of integers \(\OK = \Zomega \). The prime \(\pp = (1-\omega )\) is the unique prime of \(\Zomega \) above \(3\), with \(\Nf (\pp ) = 3\) and \((3) = \pp ^2\). We write \(\varepsilon = \chi _{-3} = \bigl (\tfrac {-3}{\cdot }\bigr )\) for the non-principal character modulo \(3\), which is the quadratic character of \(\K /\Q \). For a Dirichlet character \(\chi \), \(\cond (\chi )\) is its conductor. All constants are absolute and eﬀectively computable unless stated otherwise.

2. Proof of Condition W3

Lemma 2.1 (Real characters modulo \(3^K\)). For \(K \geq 1\), the group \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) is cyclic of order \(2\cdot 3^{K-1}\), and there is exactly one real non-principal character modulo \(3^K\), namely the character induced by \(\chi _{-3}\). Its \(L\)-function is \(L(s,\chi _{-3})\). In particular, for \(K \geq 2\) every primitive character modulo \(3^K\) is complex.

Proof. The powers of \(2\) modulo \(9\) are \(2, 4, 8, 7, 5, 1\), so \(2\) is a primitive root modulo \(9\), hence modulo every \(3^K\) [4, Ch. 4] (and trivially modulo \(3\)). The character group of a cyclic group of even order is cyclic of the same order and has exactly one element of order \(2\). The character induced by \(\chi _{-3}\) is real and non-principal, so it is that element. Since \(\chi _{-3}(n) = 0\) already when \(3 \mid n\), the induced character coincides with \(\chi _{-3}\) as a function on \(\Z \), and the two \(L\)-functions are equal. Its conductor is \(3\), so it is not primitive modulo \(3^K\) for \(K \geq 2\). □

Lemma 2.2 (Positivity of \(L(\sigma ,\chi _{-3})\)). \(L(\sigma ,\chi _{-3}) > 0\) for every real \(\sigma > 0\).

Proof. For \(\sigma > 0\) the series \(\sum _n \chi _{-3}(n)n^{-\sigma }\) converges, and grouping consecutive non-zero terms gives \[ L(\sigma ,\chi _{-3}) = \sum _{m \geq 0}\bigl ((3m+1)^{-\sigma } - (3m+2)^{-\sigma }\bigr ), \] a series of positive terms. □

For example, \(L(0,\chi _{-3}) = 1/3\) and \(L(1,\chi _{-3}) = \pi /(3\sqrt 3) = 0.6045\ldots \).

Theorem 2.3 (Classical zero-free region; see [2, Ch. 14]). There is an absolute, eﬀectively computable constant \(c_0 \in (0,1]\) with the following property. Let \(q \geq 3\) and let \(\chi \) be a non-principal character modulo \(q\). If \(\chi \) is not real, \(L(s,\chi )\) has no zero in the region \(\sigma \geq 1 - c_0/\log \bigl (q(|t|+2)\bigr )\). If \(\chi \) is real, \(L(s,\chi )\) has at most one zero in this region, and such a zero is real and simple.

Proof of Theorem 1.1. Let \(c = c_0/2\), with \(c_0\) as in Theorem 2.3, and suppose that \(\beta \) is a real zero of \(L(s,\chi )\) with \(\beta > 1 - c/\log q\). Since \(L(s,\chi ) \neq 0\) for \(\sigma \geq 1\), we have \(\beta < 1\). Since \(q \geq 3\), \(\log (2q) \leq 2\log q\), so \(\beta > 1 - c_0/\log (2q)\), and \(s = \beta \) lies in the region of Theorem 2.3 (with \(t = 0\)).

If \(K \geq 2\), then \(\chi \) is not real (Lemma 2.1), and Theorem 2.3 excludes \(\beta \). If \(K = 1\), the only primitive character modulo \(3\) is \(\chi = \chi _{-3}\), and \(L(\beta ,\chi _{-3}) > 0\) by Lemma 2.2. In either case we reach a contradiction. □

Remark 2.4 (The one-line argument, and a slightly stronger statement). In one line: \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) is cyclic of even order, so the only real non-principal character modulo \(3^K\) is induced from \(\chi _{-3}\); an exceptional zero can only belong to a real character; so any exceptional zero modulo \(3^K\) would be a zero of the single ﬁxed function \(L(s,\chi _{-3})\), which is positive on \((0,1]\). The same argument shows that no non-principal character modulo \(3^K\), primitive or not, has a zero in the region of Theorem 2.3 with \(q = 3^K\): the moduli \(3^K\) have no exceptional zero. Consequently the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions to these moduli holds in the Siegel–Walﬁsz range with eﬀectively computable constants [2, Ch. 20].

Remark 2.5 (Nothing here is speciﬁc to \(3\)). The proof uses only that \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) is cyclic of even order and that one ﬁxed real \(L\)-function has no real zero near \(1\). For every odd prime \(\ell \), \((\Z /\ell ^K\Z )^\times \) is cyclic of even order, and the only real non-principal character modulo \(\ell ^K\) is induced from the Legendre symbol \((\cdot /\ell )\). Hence, for each ﬁxed \(\ell \), the same argument proves Condition W3 for the moduli \(\ell ^K\), with a constant \(c_\ell > 0\) that depends on \(\ell \) (through the largest real zero of \(L(s,(\cdot /\ell ))\) in \((0,1)\), if there is one). A constant uniform in \(\ell \) would exclude Siegel zeros for the real characters of prime conductor, which is an open problem. Likewise, the ﬁeld \(\Qomega \) plays no special role: \(\Q (\sqrt {-7})\), \(\Q (\sqrt {-11})\), \(\Q (\sqrt {-19})\), \(\Q (\sqrt {-43})\), \(\Q (\sqrt {-67})\) and \(\Q (\sqrt {-163})\) also have class number \(1\), and each is ramiﬁed at a single prime \(\ell \), with \((\ell ) = \mathfrak l^2\).

3. The ﬁeld \(\Qomega \) (context)

Proposition 3.1 (Arithmetic of \(\Qomega \)). The ﬁeld \(\K = \Qomega = \Q (\sqrt {-3})\) is imaginary quadratic, hence a CM ﬁeld; its maximal totally real subﬁeld is \(\Q \). Its ring of integers is \(\Zomega \), its discriminant is \(-3\), its class number is \(h_\K = 1\), and its unit group is \(\mu _6 = \{\pm 1, \pm \omega , \pm \omega ^2\}\).

Proof. Since \(-3 \equiv 1 \pmod 4\), the ring of integers is \(\Z \bigl [\tfrac {1+\sqrt {-3}}{2}\bigr ] = \Zomega \), with discriminant \(-3\). The Minkowski bound equals \((2/\pi )\sqrt {3} \approx 1.10\), so every ideal class contains an integral ideal of norm at most \(1.10\), that is, of norm \(1\); hence \(h_\K = 1\). The units are the six solutions of \(a^2 - ab + b^2 = 1\), namely \(\pm 1, \pm \omega , \pm \omega ^2\). □

Remark 3.2. \(\K \) is one of the nine imaginary quadratic ﬁelds of class number one, \(\Q (\sqrt {-d})\) with \(d \in \{1,2,3,7,11,19,43,67,163\}\), as proved by Heegner [3], Baker [1] and Stark [7]. That \(\Zomega \) is a principal ideal domain was known long before; the class-number-one theorem plays no role in this paper.

Proposition 3.3 (Ramiﬁcation of \(3\)). In \(\Zomega \), the rational prime \(3\) ramiﬁes: \((3) = \pp ^2\) where \(\pp = (1-\omega )\) and \(\Nf (\pp ) = 3\).

Proof. \(\Nf (1-\omega ) = (1-\omega )(1-\bar \omega ) = 1 - (\omega + \bar \omega ) + \omega \bar \omega = 1 - (-1) + 1 = 3\); equivalently, \(\Nf (a+b\omega ) = a^2-ab+b^2\) gives \(\Nf (1-\omega ) = 1+1+1 = 3\). Since \(1+\omega +\omega ^2=0\), we have \(3 = -\omega ^2(1-\omega )^2\), and \(-\omega ^2 \in \Zomega ^\times \). □

Remark 3.4. The prime \(3\) is the only rational prime that ramiﬁes in \(\K \): a prime \(\ell \) ramiﬁes in \(\K \) if and only if \(\ell \mid \disc (\K ) = -3\) [6]. This is not special to \(3\): in the same way, \(\ell \) is the only prime ramiﬁed in \(\Q (\sqrt {-\ell })\) for \(\ell = 7, 11, 19, 43, 67, 163\) (Remark 2.5).

Lemma 3.5 (Splitting of primes \(\ell \neq 3\)). A prime \(\ell \neq 3\) splits in \(\K \) into two distinct primes of norm \(\ell \) if \(\ell \equiv 1 \pmod 3\), and is inert, with \(\Zomega /(\ell ) \cong \F _{\ell ^2}\), if \(\ell \equiv 2 \pmod 3\). In particular \(2\) is inert and \(\Zomega /(2) \cong \F _4\).

Proof. Since \(\Zomega \cong \Z [x]/(x^2+x+1)\), the prime \(\ell \) splits if \(x^2 + x + 1\) has a root modulo \(\ell \) and is inert otherwise (the Dedekind–Kummer criterion [6]; for \(\ell \neq 3\) there is no repeated root, as the discriminant of \(x^2+x+1\) is \(-3\)). A root \(r\) satisﬁes \(r^3 = 1\) and \(r \neq 1\) (as \(3 \not \equiv 0 \pmod \ell \)), so a root exists if and only if \(\F _\ell ^\times \) has an element of order \(3\), that is, if and only if \(3 \mid \ell - 1\). For \(\ell = 2\) this agrees with the usual criterion for \(2\) in \(\Q (\sqrt d)\) with \(d \equiv 1 \pmod 4\) (\(2\) splits if \(d \equiv 1 \pmod 8\) and is inert if \(d \equiv 5 \pmod 8\)), since \(-3 \equiv 5 \pmod 8\). □

4. Base change of characters modulo \(3^K\) (context)

4.1. Hecke characters of ﬁnite order. Let \(\mathfrak m\) be a nonzero ideal of \(\OK \). Since \(\K \) is imaginary quadratic, a Hecke character of ﬁnite order of \(\K \) with modulus \(\mathfrak m\) is a homomorphism \(\Psi \) from the group of fractional ideals of \(\K \) prime to \(\mathfrak m\) to \(\C ^\times \) such that \(\Psi ((\alpha )) = 1\) for every \(\alpha \in \OK \) with \(\alpha \equiv 1 \pmod {\mathfrak m}\); that is, a character of the ray class group modulo \(\mathfrak m\). Its conductor \(\cond (\Psi )\) is the smallest such modulus. We put \(\Psi (\mathfrak a) = 0\) for integral ideals \(\mathfrak a\) not prime to \(\mathfrak m\), and \[ L(s, \Psi ) = \sum _{\mathfrak a} \Psi (\mathfrak a)\Nf (\mathfrak a)^{-s} = \prod _{\qq } \bigl (1 - \Psi (\qq )\,\Nf (\qq )^{-s}\bigr )^{-1}, \qquad \Re s > 1, \] where \(\mathfrak a\) runs over nonzero integral ideals and \(\qq \) over prime ideals.

4.2. The base change \(\chi \circ \Nf \).

Proposition 4.1 (Base change of characters modulo \(3^K\)). Let \(K \geq 1\), let \(\chi \) be a Dirichlet character modulo \(3^K\), and put \(\Psi _\chi (\mathfrak a) = \chi (\Nf \mathfrak a)\) for nonzero integral ideals \(\mathfrak a\) of \(\Zomega \) (so \(\Psi _\chi (\mathfrak a) = 0\) if \(\pp \mid \mathfrak a\)).

(i)
\(\Psi _\chi \) is a Hecke character of ﬁnite order with modulus \(\pp ^{2K}\), and \(\Psi _\chi ((n)) = \chi (n)^2\) for every \(n \in \Z \).
(ii)
The image of the norm map \((\OK /\pp ^{2K})^\times \to (\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) is the subgroup \(\{n \equiv 1 \pmod 3\}\), of index \(2\). Consequently \(\Psi _\chi = \Psi _{\chi \varepsilon }\), and the map \(\chi \mapsto \Psi _\chi \) is two-to-one.
(iii)
If \(\chi \) is primitive, then \(\Psi _\chi \) is the trivial character when \(K = 1\), and \(\cond (\Psi _\chi ) = \pp ^{2K-1}\) when \(K \geq 2\).

Proof. (i) The norm is multiplicative, so \(\Psi _\chi \) is multiplicative, and since units have norm \(1\), \(\Psi _\chi ((\alpha )) = \chi (\Nf \alpha )\) does not depend on the generator \(\alpha \). If \(\alpha \in \OK \) and \(\alpha \equiv 1 \pmod {\pp ^{2K}}\), then \(\alpha = 1 + 3^K\beta \) with \(\beta \in \OK \), and \(\Nf (\alpha ) = 1 + 3^K\operatorname {Tr}(\beta ) + 3^{2K}\Nf (\beta ) \equiv 1 \pmod {3^K}\), so \(\Psi _\chi ((\alpha )) = 1\). Finally \(\Nf ((n)) = n^2\).

(ii) For \(\alpha = a + b\omega \) we have \(\Nf (\alpha ) = a^2 - ab + b^2 \equiv (a+b)^2 \pmod 3\), so every norm prime to \(3\) is \(\equiv 1 \pmod 3\). Conversely, the squares in the cyclic group \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) of order \(2\cdot 3^{K-1}\) form its unique subgroup of index \(2\), which is therefore \(\{n \equiv 1 \pmod 3\}\), and \(m^2 = \Nf (m)\) for \(m \in \Z \). Hence the image is exactly \(\{n \equiv 1 \pmod 3\}\). Two characters modulo \(3^K\) agree on this subgroup if and only if they are equal or diﬀer by the unique non-trivial character trivial on it, which is \(\varepsilon \); this gives the last two assertions.

(iii) For \(K = 1\), \(\chi = \varepsilon \), and \(\varepsilon (\Nf \mathfrak a) = 1\) for every \(\mathfrak a\) prime to \(\pp \), by (ii). Let \(K \geq 2\). One checks that \(\operatorname {Tr}(\OK ) = \Z \) and \(\operatorname {Tr}(\pp ) = 3\Z \) (indeed \(\operatorname {Tr}((1-\omega )(a+b\omega )) = 3a\)); since \(\pp ^{2j} = 3^j\OK \), it follows that \(\operatorname {Tr}(\pp ^m) = 3^{\lceil m/2\rceil }\Z \). If \(\alpha = 1 + \beta \) with \(\beta \in \pp ^{2K-1}\), then \(\Nf (\alpha ) = 1 + \operatorname {Tr}(\beta ) + \Nf (\beta ) \equiv 1 \pmod {3^K}\), because \(\operatorname {Tr}(\beta ) \in 3^K\Z \) and \(\Nf (\beta ) \in 3^{2K-1}\Z \). So \(\pp ^{2K-1}\) is a modulus for \(\Psi _\chi \). On the other hand, for \(t \in \Z \) the element \(\alpha = 1 + 3^{K-1}t\) is \(\equiv 1 \pmod {\pp ^{2K-2}}\), and \(\Nf (\alpha ) = (1+3^{K-1}t)^2 \equiv 1 + 2\cdot 3^{K-1}t \pmod {3^K}\) because \(2K - 2 \geq K\); these values run through all classes \(\equiv 1 \pmod {3^{K-1}}\). A primitive \(\chi \) is non-trivial on that subgroup, so \(\pp ^{2K-2}\) is not a modulus for \(\Psi _\chi \), and \(\cond (\Psi _\chi ) = \pp ^{2K-1}\). □

The conductor in (iii) agrees with the conductor–discriminant formula \(|\disc (\K )|\cdot \Nf \cond (\Psi _\chi ) = \cond (\chi )\,\cond (\chi \varepsilon )\), which for \(K \geq 2\) reads \(3 \cdot 3^{2K-1} = 3^K\cdot 3^K\).

Remark 4.2 (The lifting identity of the original version). The original version (former Proposition 2.5) claimed, for every primitive \(\chi \) modulo \(3^K\), a primitive Hecke character \(\Psi _\chi \) of conductor \(\pp ^{2K}\) with \(\chi (n) = \Psi _\chi ((n))\) for all \(n\) prime to \(3\). No such character exists when \(\chi \) is odd: since \((n) = (-n)\), any function of ideals takes the same value at \(n\) and \(-n\), which forces \(\chi (-1) = 1\). Half of the characters modulo \(3^K\) are odd, because \(-1\) is the element of order \(2\) of the cyclic group \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \). The construction actually given was \(\Psi _\chi = \chi \circ \Nf \), for which (i) gives \(\chi (n)^2\); its conductor is \(\pp ^{2K-1}\), not \(\pp ^{2K}\), by (iii); and the norm map is not surjective, by (ii). In particular the ramiﬁcation of \(3\) does not make conductors “match”. The opposite is true: for a prime \(\ell \neq 3\), which is unramiﬁed, the base change of a primitive character modulo \(\ell ^K\) has conductor exactly \(\ell ^K\OK \), whereas at the ramiﬁed prime \(3\) the conductor drops to \(\pp ^{2K-1}\).

4.3. The \(L\)-function factorisation.

Lemma 4.3 (\(L\)-function factorisation). For every Dirichlet character \(\chi \) modulo \(3^K\), \(K \geq 1\), \[ L(s, \Psi _\chi ) = L(s, \chi ) \cdot L(s, \chi \varepsilon ) \qquad (\Re s > 1), \] and hence for all \(s\), by analytic continuation.

Proof. Both sides are Euler products; we compare the local factors at each rational prime \(\ell \).

\(\ell = 3\). \(\Psi _\chi (\pp ) = \chi (3) = 0\), and \(\chi (3) = \chi \varepsilon (3) = 0\), so both local factors are \(1\).

\(\ell \equiv 1 \pmod {3}\) (split). Write \(\ell \Zomega = \qq _1\qq _2\) with \(\qq _1 \neq \qq _2\) of norm \(\ell \). The local factor of \(L(s, \Psi _\chi )\) is \((1-\chi (\ell )\ell ^{-s})^{-2}\). Since \(\varepsilon (\ell ) = 1\), this equals the product of the local factors of \(L(s,\chi )\) and \(L(s,\chi \varepsilon )\).

\(\ell \equiv 2 \pmod {3}\) (inert, including \(\ell = 2\)). Then \(\ell \Zomega = \qq \) is a prime of norm \(\ell ^2\), and \(\Psi _\chi (\qq ) = \chi (\ell ^2) = \chi (\ell )^2\). Since \(\varepsilon (\ell ) = -1\), the product of the local factors of \(L(s,\chi )\) and \(L(s,\chi \varepsilon )\) is \((1-\chi (\ell )\ell ^{-s})^{-1}(1+\chi (\ell )\ell ^{-s})^{-1} = (1-\chi (\ell )^2\ell ^{-2s})^{-1}\), the local factor of \(L(s,\Psi _\chi )\). □

Remark 4.4 (The twist \(\chi \varepsilon \), and the case \(K = 1\)). For \(K \geq 2\) and \(\chi \) primitive, \(\chi \varepsilon \) is again primitive modulo \(3^K\), since \(\varepsilon \) is trivial on \(\{n \equiv 1 \pmod {3^{K-1}}\}\), and \(\Psi _{\chi \varepsilon } = \Psi _\chi \). For \(K = 1\), \(\chi = \varepsilon \), and \(\chi \varepsilon = \varepsilon ^2\) is the principal character modulo \(3\), of conductor \(1\). Then \(\Psi _\varepsilon \) is trivial, and Lemma 4.3 reads \(\zeta _\K (s)(1 - 3^{-s}) = L(s,\varepsilon )\,\zeta (s)(1-3^{-s})\), that is, \(\zeta _\K (s) = \zeta (s)L(s,\varepsilon )\). The original version stated that \(\chi \varepsilon \) is non-principal of conductor \(3^K\) for all \(K \geq 1\), and that \(\Psi _\varepsilon \) is primitive of conductor \(\pp ^2\); both are false for \(K = 1\). Indeed the ray class group of \(\K \) modulo \(\pp ^2\) is trivial, since the six units map onto \((\OK /\pp ^2)^\times \), which has order \(6\); so no non-trivial character of conductor \(\pp ^2\) exists.

Remark 4.5. Lemma 4.3 expresses \(L(s,\Psi _\chi )\) through two Dirichlet \(L\)-functions modulo \(3^K\). A zero-free region for \(L(s,\Psi _\chi )\) is therefore the same statement as a zero-free region for the pair \(L(s,\chi )\), \(L(s,\chi \varepsilon )\), and the passage to \(\Qomega \) adds nothing to Section 2.

5. The former “Stark–Baker theorem” (withdrawn)

The original version derived Theorem 1.1 from the following statement, presented as a “Stark–Baker theorem” (former Theorem 3.2): for every CM ﬁeld \(F\) and every Hecke character \(\Psi \) of \(F\) of conductor \(\mathfrak f\), \(L(s,\Psi )\) has no real zero in \(\sigma > 1 - c_F/\log (\Nf (\mathfrak f)\,|\disc (F)|)\), with \(c_F > 0\) eﬀective and depending only on \([F:\Q ]\). This is not a known theorem, and it is withdrawn, together with its proof sketch and the former Remark 3.3 and Corollary 3.4.

As stated it would solve the Landau–Siegel zero problem. Take \(F = \Q (\sqrt {-d})\), any imaginary quadratic ﬁeld (hence a CM ﬁeld of degree \(2\)), and \(\Psi \) trivial. Then \(L(s,\Psi ) = \zeta _F(s) = \zeta (s)L(s,\chi _{d_F})\), where \(d_F\) is the discriminant of \(F\) and \(\chi _{d_F}\) the associated quadratic character, and \(\zeta (\sigma ) < 0\) for \(0 < \sigma < 1\). The statement would therefore give that \(L(s,\chi _{d_F})\) has no real zero in \(\sigma > 1 - c/\log |d_F|\) for every imaginary quadratic ﬁeld, with \(c\) absolute and eﬀective. That is the open Landau–Siegel problem for these characters; via the class number formula it would also give an eﬀective lower bound \(h(d_F) \gg |d_F|^{1/2}/\log |d_F|\), far beyond the known eﬀective lower bounds.

What Heegner [3], Baker [1] and Stark [7] proved is the class-number-one theorem: the nine ﬁelds listed after Proposition 3.1 are the only imaginary quadratic ﬁelds of class number \(1\). It gives no zero-free region for Hecke \(L\)-functions of \(\Qomega \). The remark that Siegel zeros “remain possible” only for non-CM ﬁelds is also withdrawn, since every imaginary quadratic ﬁeld is a CM ﬁeld. None of this aﬀects Theorem 1.1, whose proof in Section 2 uses only the cyclicity of \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \).

6. Relation to the companion paper and to Watt’s theorem

The original Sections 5–7 (a proposition that the argument is speciﬁc to \(3\), a “coherence” proposition, a table verifying “Watt’s conditions” W1–W3, and a corollary asserting that the level of distribution \(\theta _W = 5/8\) for cascade moduli holds unconditionally) are withdrawn, for the following reasons.

(1)
Watt’s theorem has no conditions W1–W3. N. Watt [8] proves a mean-value estimate in the \(t\)-aspect for \(\int _0^T|\zeta (\tfrac 12+it)|^4\,\bigl |\sum _{m\leq M}a_m m^{-it}\bigr |^2\,dt\). It involves no family of Dirichlet characters modulo \(q\) and has no hypotheses “\(M \leq q\)”, “\(MR \leq q^2\)” or “no Siegel zeros”. Moreover, with the parameters \(M = Q^{2/3}\) and \(R = Q^{1/3}\) used in the original version of [10], “W1” fails for every \(3^K < Q^{2/3}\) and “W2” for every \(3^K < Q^{1/2}\), so these were not veriﬁed for the family either (former Theorem 7.1).
(2)
W3 is unrelated to \(\theta \leq 7/32\). The bound \(\theta \leq 7/32\) is the Kim–Sarnak bound [5] \(\lambda _1 \geq 1/4 - (7/64)^2\) towards Selberg’s eigenvalue conjecture, written in the normalisation \(\theta = \sqrt {1 - 4\lambda _1}\). It is an unconditional theorem about Maass forms of every level, and neither the absence of Siegel zeros nor any zero-density estimate implies it. The derivation of it in the original version is withdrawn, together with the zero-density estimate quoted there, whose exponent and source we could not conﬁrm.
(3)
W3 makes nothing unconditional. Theorem 1.1 of [10], a sum over the moduli \(3^K \leq x^\theta \), holds for every ﬁxed \(\theta < 1\) by the trivial bound and the Siegel–Walﬁsz theorem. It does not need W3, although Remark 2.4 makes its implied constant eﬀective. The per-modulus statement for \(3^K\) up to \(x^{5/8-\varepsilon }\) is a conjecture in [10], which would go beyond the Generalised Riemann Hypothesis. A zero-free region of width \(c/\log q\) cannot help with it: for \(q = x^\theta \) one has \(x^{1 - c/\log q} = e^{-c/\theta }x\), so such a region gives no saving for moduli of size a power of \(x\). The former Corollary 7.2 is therefore withdrawn. (Its reference to an annotation “[NOTE]” in [10] was also wrong: there is none.)
(4)
No “coherence”. The former Proposition 6.1 asserted that one fact, the ramiﬁcation \((3) = \pp ^2\), drives three steps of [10]: a three-level ﬁltration, Kloosterman cancellation beyond the Weil bound, and a conductor match. All three are false. The \((1-\omega )\)-adic ﬁltration of \(\Zomega /(3^K)\) has \(2K\) steps, not three. The bound \(|S(a,b;3^K)| \ll (3^K)^{3/8+\varepsilon }\) fails, since \(S(1,1;3^K) = 2\cdot 3^{K/2}\cos (4\pi /3^K)\) for even \(K \geq 2\) (see [10]); and cyclicity of \((\Z /\ell ^K\Z )^\times \) holds for every odd prime \(\ell \) and has nothing to do with \(\Zomega \). The conductor does not match (Remark 4.2).
(5)
Not speciﬁc to \(3\). The former Proposition 5.1 is withdrawn; see Remark 2.5. Its proof also stated that \(2\) splits in \(\K \) “since \(-3 \equiv 1 \pmod 8\)”. In fact \(-3 \equiv 5 \pmod 8\), and \(2\) is inert (Lemma 3.5). Its third property, that characters of \((\Z /3^K\Z )^\times \) correspond precisely to Hecke characters of \(\K \) of conductor \(\pp ^{2K}\), is false as well: the map \(\chi \mapsto \chi \circ \Nf \) is two-to-one (Proposition 4.1(ii)), and the ray class group of \(\K \) modulo \(\pp ^{2K}\) has order \(3^{2K-2}\), not \(2\cdot 3^{K-1}\).
