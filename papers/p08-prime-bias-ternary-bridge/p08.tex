\begin{abstract}
In 2016 Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan observed that consecutive primes are biased
against repeating their residue modulo~$q$, for instance their last decimal digit. In the
same paper they explained the bias by a heuristic built on the Hardy--Littlewood prime
$k$-tuples conjecture, with explicit lower-order terms whose leading part has size
$\log\log x/\log x$. The first version of the present paper proposed a different,
``ternary constitutional'' mechanism: that the bias is the base-10 projection of the
mod~3 constraint on primes, that the $p=3$ factor of the singular series vanishes for
gaps divisible by~3, and that the slow decay of the bias is the Siegel--Walfi{}sz rate. We
test these claims against a recomputation of the consecutive-prime transition counts for
the first $10^8$ and $10^9$ primes. All three are false. The local factor at $p = 3$ is
$3/2$, its maximum, for gaps divisible by~3 and $3/4$ otherwise; gap~6 is the most
common gap; $\mathfrak{S}(2)$ is the smallest even-gap singular-series value, not the
largest; the last digit of a prime carries no information about its residue mod~3; and the
bias is about the same modulo~4 as modulo~3. A simple Hardy--Littlewood model with the
correct $p = 3$ factor reproduces the base-10 transition matrix to $0.85$ percentage
points (RMS); the factor claimed in the first version gives $5.9$ points and largely
removes the diagonal suppression. We conclude that the Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan explanation
accounts for the bias, and that the ternary-constitutional explanation, as originally
stated, is not supported. The prime~3 enters only in the ordinary way, through the
singular series, which weights gaps divisible by~3 twice as heavily as other gaps.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Revision note (November 2026).}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

In 2016 Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan~\cite{LOS16} published a result that anyone
with a computer can check. If the last digits of consecutive primes were independent,
each of the four digits $\{1, 3, 7, 9\}$ would follow any other about 25\% of the time.
They do not. Among the first $10^8$ primes, a prime ending in~1 is followed by another
prime ending in~1 only 18.5\% of the time, and among the first $10^9$ primes a prime
ending in~9 is followed by a prime ending in~1 about 65\% more often than by another
prime ending in~9 (Section~\ref{sec:findings}). The same kind of bias is observed for
other moduli (Section~\ref{sec:bases}), and it is predicted for every modulus $q \geq 3$,
not only for last digits (which are residues modulo~10). It fades as the
primes grow, but slowly: ``at a snail's pace'', in the words of Quanta
Magazine~\cite{Kla16}.

Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan also explained the bias, in the same paper. From the
Hardy--Littlewood prime $k$-tuples conjecture~\cite{HL23}, together with uniformity
assumptions, they derived a conjectural asymptotic formula for the number of
consecutive primes in each pair of residue classes modulo~$q$. Its second-order terms
are explicit. The leading correction has size $\log\log x/\log x$ and depends only on
$\varphi(q)$ and on whether the two classes coincide; this is why the bias appears for
every modulus $q \geq 3$ and why it decays slowly. The argument is a heuristic, not a proof, since it
rests on the $k$-tuples conjecture. But it is quantitative, and it fits the data well.

The first version of this paper proposed a different explanation: that the bias is
``the base-10 projection of ternary constitutional pressure'', that is, of the fact that
every prime $p > 3$ lies in one of the classes $1, 2 \pmod 3$. It asserted that
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item the $p = 3$ factor of the Hardy--Littlewood singular series vanishes for gaps
    divisible by~3;
  \item gap~2 has the largest even-gap singular series and gap~10 a value below~1;
  \item the ternary class of the smallest gap compatible with a digit transition governs
    whether that transition is enhanced or suppressed; and
  \item the ``snail's pace'' is the Siegel--Walfi{}sz rate at which primes become
    equidistributed modulo~3.
\end{enumerate}
This revision checks each assertion against the singular series and against recomputed
data. All four are false (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism}--\ref{sec:validation}).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Main result (revised, informal).} \emph{The Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan
bias is accounted for by their Hardy--Littlewood heuristic. The prime~3 enters that
heuristic through its local factor, $3/2$ for gaps divisible by~3 and $3/4$ for other gaps,
which favours gaps divisible by~3. This factor visibly shapes the base-10 transition
matrix, but it is one local factor among many, and it is not what produces the diagonal
suppression, which is an effect of gap length. The
ternary-constitutional mechanism of the first version is not supported by the data.}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Organisation.} Section~\ref{sec:findings} recalls the Lemke
Oliver--Soundararajan findings, with recomputed data. Section~\ref{sec:framework} records
the elementary mod~3 facts. Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} states the singular-series facts
correctly and tests the first version's mechanism against the data.
Section~\ref{sec:snail} discusses the rate of decay. Section~\ref{sec:validation} treats base
dependence, the parity barrier, and the relation to the Constitutional Forcing programme.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{The Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan Findings}
\label{sec:findings}
% ============================================================

\subsection{The Observation}

For consecutive primes $p_n$ and $p_{n+1}$, consider the frequency with which the
last digit of~$p_n$ is followed by each possible last digit of~$p_{n+1}$. The last digits
of primes greater than~5 are drawn from $\{1, 3, 7, 9\}$, and if consecutive primes
behaved independently each transition would occur with frequency about $1/4 = 25\%$.
Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan~\cite{LOS16} tabulated these transitions for the first
$10^8$ primes.

Table~\ref{tab:matrix} gives the matrix recomputed with a segmented sieve over the
first $10^9$ primes. The sieve reproduces the standard values of $\pi(10^k)$ for
$6 \leq k \leq 10$, of $p_{10^k}$ for $6 \leq k \leq 9$, and the twin-prime count
$\pi_2(10^8) = 440{,}312$; for the first $10^8$ primes its counts for the row ``ends in~1''
agree with the published counts of~\cite{LOS16} to within one, the difference being an
endpoint convention. (The code and raw output are kept with the programme's audit
material, \texttt{audit/scripts/p08}.) Each row of the $10^8$ block contains about
$2.5 \times 10^7$ transitions, and each row of the $10^9$ block about $2.5 \times 10^8$.

The headline finding is that \emph{consecutive primes avoid repeating their last digit}:
every diagonal entry is below 25\%. Among the first $10^8$ primes the suppression is
strongest for the digits 3 and~7 (17.8\%) and weakest for 1 and~9 (18.5\%). The ratio
of $9 \to 1$ to $9 \to 9$ is $1.73$ for the first $10^8$ primes and $1.65$ for the first
$10^9$; that is the ``65\% more often'' of Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Relative to the uniform
25\%, $9 \to 1$ is enhanced by a factor $1.28$ (first $10^8$ primes).

The matrix is also symmetric under $(a \to b) \mapsto (-b \to -a) \pmod{10}$: for
instance $3 \to 1$ is 24.04\% and $9 \to 7$ is 24.05\%. Corresponding entries agree to
within $0.02$ percentage points for the first $10^8$ primes. This is the symmetry
$n \mapsto -n$, under which Hardy--Littlewood densities are invariant.

The first version of this paper printed a different matrix, described as approximate
data for the first billion primes and attributed to~\cite{LOS16}. Only its first row was
close to the data. Its rows for the digits 3, 7 and~9 were wrong by as much as 14
percentage points (for example $3 \to 1$ was given as 38\% and $3 \to 9$ as 20\%, against
24.04\% and 30.01\%), and they violated the symmetry just described. That matrix,
and every conclusion drawn from it, has been withdrawn.

\subsection{The Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan Explanation}

Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan derived the bias from the Hardy--Littlewood prime
$k$-tuples conjecture~\cite{HL23}, which predicts the density of prime pairs $(p, p+h)$
through the singular series $\mathfrak{S}(h)$ (Section~\ref{sec:singular}). Let
$\pi(x; q, (a,b))$ count the primes $p_n \leq x$ with $p_n \equiv a$ and
$p_{n+1} \equiv b \pmod q$. Their conjecture~\cite{LOS16} reads
\[
  \pi(x; q, (a,b)) = \frac{\operatorname{li}(x)}{\varphi(q)^2}
  \left(1 + c_1(q;a,b)\,\frac{\log\log x}{\log x}
  + c_2(q;a,b)\,\frac{1}{\log x}
  + O\!\left((\log x)^{-7/4}\right)\right),
\]
with $c_1(q;a,b) = 1/2$ for $a \not\equiv b$ and
$c_1(q;a,a) = 1/2 - \varphi(q)/2$. The coefficient $c_2(q;a,b)$ is explicit; it is
computed from the Hardy--Littlewood singular series and depends on the pair $(a,b)$. The
leading correction is negative on the diagonal for every $q \geq 3$, which is the diagonal
suppression; it depends only on $\varphi(q)$, which is why the effect looks alike for
different moduli; and it decays like $\log\log x/\log x$, which is the slow decay. The finer
structure of the off-diagonal entries comes from~$c_2$. Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan
studied the distribution of these biases further in~\cite{LOS17}.

Their argument is a heuristic: it assumes the $k$-tuples conjecture in a uniform form.
It is nevertheless quantitative and it agrees well with the data, and nothing in the
present paper supplies an alternative to it.

\section{The Ternary Constitutional Framework}
\label{sec:framework}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Core Definitions}

We work with remainder classes modulo~3. Every integer $n$ belongs to exactly one of
three classes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $n \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$: the \textbf{Forbidden Class} (divisible by~3);
  \item $n \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$: \textbf{Constitutional Class~I};
  \item $n \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$: \textbf{Constitutional Class~II}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{definition}[Constitutional Constraint]
\label{def:constraint}
A prime $p > 3$ is \emph{constitutionally constrained}: it must satisfy
$p \equiv 1$ or $p \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$. The Forbidden Class is closed to primes
greater than~3.
\end{definition}

This is elementary: any integer $n > 3$ with $n \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$ is divisible by~3
and hence composite. Therefore all primes $p > 3$ lie in
$\{p : p \equiv 1 \text{ or } 2 \pmod{3}\}$.

\subsection{The Twin Prime Constitutional Signature}

The following fact is classical and elementary.

\begin{theorem}[Constitutional Signature]
\label{thm:signature}
Let $(p, p+2)$ be a twin prime pair with $p > 3$. Then
\[
  p \equiv 2 \pmod{3} \quad \text{and} \quad p + 2 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Among any three consecutive integers, exactly one is divisible by~3.
\begin{itemize}
  \item If $p \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$: then $p = 3$ (the only such prime), excluded.
  \item If $p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$: then $p + 2 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$, so $p + 2 > 3$ is
    composite. Contradiction.
  \item If $p \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$: then $p + 2 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$. Neither $p$ nor
    $p + 2$ is divisible by~3. Both may be prime. \qedhere
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
There are $3{,}424{,}506$ twin prime pairs $(p, p+2)$ with $p \leq 10^9$. The pair
$(3,5)$ is excluded by the hypothesis $p > 3$; the remaining $3{,}424{,}505$ pairs all
have $p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$, as the theorem requires. The count is a check on the
computation, not additional evidence for a two-line proof.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
\label{rem:gaps-mod3}
The same argument applies to any even gap~$h$. If $3 \nmid h$, a pair of primes
$(p, p+h)$ with $p > 3$ is possible for only one class of $p \bmod 3$ (namely
$p \equiv 2$ if $h \equiv 2$, and $p \equiv 1$ if $h \equiv 1 \pmod 3$). If $3 \mid h$,
both classes are possible. Gaps divisible by~3 are therefore \emph{less} constrained
modulo~3, not forbidden. This is the source of the $p = 3$ factor in the singular series
(Section~\ref{sec:singular}).
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Ternary Equilibrium}

\begin{theorem}[Ternary Equilibrium; de la Vall\'ee Poussin~\cite{dlVP96}]
\label{thm:equilibrium}
Let $\pi_1(N)$ and $\pi_2(N)$ denote the number of primes $p \leq N$ with
$p \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$ and $p \equiv 2 \pmod{3}$ respectively. Then
\[
  \frac{\pi_1(N)}{\pi(N)} \to \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{and} \quad
  \frac{\pi_2(N)}{\pi(N)} \to \frac{1}{2} \quad \text{as } N \to \infty.
\]
\end{theorem}

This is the case $q = 3$ of the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, proved
by de la Vall\'ee Poussin~\cite{dlVP96}. Dirichlet~\cite{Dir37} had proved earlier that
each of the two classes contains infinitely many primes.

\begin{remark}[Last digits carry no ternary information]
\label{rem:crt}
Since $\gcd(10, 3) = 1$, the Chinese remainder theorem identifies the reduced residues
modulo~30 with pairs (reduced residue mod~10, reduced residue mod~3). By the prime
number theorem for arithmetic progressions modulo~30, each of the eight reduced classes
mod~30 contains asymptotically $1/8$ of the primes. Hence the last digit of a prime and
its class modulo~3 are asymptotically independent: a prime ending in~3 or~9 is no more
likely than any other to be $\equiv 1$ or $\equiv 2 \pmod 3$, and none is $\equiv 0$
except~3 itself. In particular, the last digit is not a ``projection'' of the class
modulo~3.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Minimum Gaps and the Withdrawn Mechanism}

Each last-digit transition $(d_1 \to d_2)$ has a \emph{minimum possible gap}
$\mgap{d_1}{d_2}$: the smallest positive even integer $g$ with
$d_1 + g \equiv d_2 \pmod{10}$. Since all prime gaps for $p > 5$ are even, the possible
gaps for the transition are exactly $g, g + 10, g + 20, \ldots$ The values are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:mingaps}; this part of the first version was correct.

\begin{erratumbox}
\noindent The first version claimed that the ternary class of $\mgap{d_1}{d_2}$
governs whether the transition is enhanced, suppressed or ``redistributed'': gap-2
transitions enhanced because they carry the twin-prime signature, same-digit transitions
suppressed because $\mathfrak{S}(10)$ is the smallest singular series, and gap-6
transitions redistributed because the $p = 3$ Euler factor vanishes for gaps divisible
by~3. Each of these three statements is false
(Sections~\ref{sec:singular}--\ref{sec:model}), and the rule as a whole is refuted by the
data.
\end{erratumbox}

\subsection{The Singular Series and the Factor at \texorpdfstring{$p = 3$}{p = 3}}
\label{sec:singular}

For even $h > 0$, the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture~\cite{HL23} predicts
\[
  \#\{p \leq x : p \text{ and } p + h \text{ prime}\} \sim \mathfrak{S}(h)\,
  \frac{x}{(\log x)^2},
  \qquad
  \mathfrak{S}(h) = \prod_{p} \frac{1 - \nu_p(h)/p}{(1 - 1/p)^2},
\]
where $\nu_p(h)$ is the number of residue classes modulo~$p$ occupied by $\{0, h\}$:
$\nu_p(h) = 1$ if $p \mid h$ and $\nu_p(h) = 2$ otherwise. (These pairs need not be
consecutive primes.) Evaluating the local factors gives the familiar form
\[
  \mathfrak{S}(h) = 2C_2 \prod_{\substack{p \mid h \\ p > 2}} \frac{p-1}{p-2},
  \qquad
  2C_2 = 2\prod_{p > 2}\frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2} \approx 1.3203 .
\]
A local factor vanishes only when $\nu_p = p$, which for a pair happens only at $p = 2$
with $h$ odd; for even~$h$ no factor vanishes.

\begin{correctionbox}
\noindent At $p = 3$ the local factor is
\[
  \mathfrak{S}_3(h) = \frac{1 - \nu_3(h)/3}{(1 - 1/3)^2}
  = \frac{\chi_3(h)/2}{2/3}
  = \begin{cases}
    3/2 & \text{if } 3 \mid h, \\
    3/4 & \text{if } 3 \nmid h,
  \end{cases}
\]
where $\chi_3(h) = \#\{j \in \{1, 2\} : j + h \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3\}$ counts the
classes of $p \bmod 3$ for which $p + h$ is also prime to~3
(Remark~\ref{rem:gaps-mod3}). Thus $\chi_3(h)/2$ is the chance that $p + h$ is prime
to~3 given that $p$ is, and $2/3$ is the same chance for a random integer. The factor
is \emph{largest} for gaps divisible by~3, and gaps divisible by~3 receive twice the
weight of other gaps.
\end{correctionbox}

The first version wrote this factor as $(1 - 1/3)^{-1}(1 - \chi_3/\varphi(3))$ and
concluded that it vanishes for $3 \mid h$ and equals $1/2$ otherwise. That formula
inverts the role of $\chi_3$, which counts the favourable classes, and uses the
normalisation $(1-1/3)^{-1}$ instead of $(1-1/3)^{-2}$; even on its own terms it gives
$3/4$, not $1/2$, for $3 \nmid h$. It is withdrawn, together with the claim that gap-6
transitions ``map primes to the Forbidden class''.

The resulting values are
\begin{gather*}
  \mathfrak{S}(2) = \mathfrak{S}(4) = \mathfrak{S}(8) \approx 1.3203, \qquad
  \mathfrak{S}(6) = \mathfrak{S}(12) \approx 2.6406, \\
  \mathfrak{S}(10) \approx 1.7604, \qquad
  \mathfrak{S}(14) \approx 1.5844, \qquad
  \mathfrak{S}(30) \approx 3.5209 .
\end{gather*}
Since every factor $(p-1)/(p-2)$ exceeds~1, $\mathfrak{S}(2) = 2C_2$ is the
\emph{smallest} value over even~$h$, attained exactly when $h$ is a power of~2. It is
not the largest, as the first version stated, and $\mathfrak{S}(10) \approx 1.76$ is
greater than~1, not less.

The data agree. Gap~6 is the most common gap between consecutive primes among the
first $10^6$, $10^7$, $10^8$ and $10^9$ primes. Among the first $10^8$ primes it occurs
$11{,}604{,}547$ times, against $6{,}497{,}407$ for gap~2 (a ratio of $1.79$); among the
first $10^9$ primes the counts are $105{,}002{,}853$ and $58{,}047{,}180$ (ratio $1.81$).
Gaps 2 and~4, which have the same singular series, are equally common: their ratio is
$0.9998$ among the first $10^8$ primes.

\subsection{What the Transition Data Show}
\label{sec:data}

\noindent\textbf{The minimum-gap ternary class does not determine the effect.}
Averaging the observed entries of Table~\ref{tab:mingaps} by minimum gap gives
$30.47\%$ for gap~2 ($g \equiv 2 \bmod 3$), $26.39\%$ for gap~4 ($g \equiv 1$),
$29.02\%$ for gap~6 ($g \equiv 0$), $23.29\%$ for gap~8 ($g \equiv 2$) and $18.13\%$
for gap~10 ($g \equiv 1$). Gap~2 and gap~8 have the same ternary class, yet every
gap-8 transition is below 25\%. Gap~4 and gap~10 have the same class, yet gap~4 is
above 25\% and gap~10 is the most suppressed. Apart from gap~6, the averages decrease
with the size of the gap. Gap~6 is raised by the $p = 3$ factor $3/2$. This is what the
Hardy--Littlewood heuristic predicts, and it is the opposite of ``redistribution''.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Gap-6 transitions are enhanced, not redistributed.} The transitions
$1 \to 7$ (30.02\%) and $3 \to 9$ (30.01\%) are the most frequent transitions in their
rows. In particular $1 \to 7$ is slightly more frequent than the gap-2 transition
$1 \to 3$ (29.72\%; the corresponding values for the first $10^9$ primes are 29.43\%
and 29.30\%).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Gap-2 transitions are not dominated by twin primes.} The three
transitions $9 \to 1$, $1 \to 3$ and $7 \to 9$ have the same set of possible gaps,
$\{2, 12, 22, \ldots\}$. Among the first $10^8$ primes only 27.09\% of the $9 \to 1$
transitions have gap~2, while 38.06\% have gap~12, which is divisible by~3; for
$1 \to 3$ the shares are 29.15\% and 37.56\%, and for $7 \to 9$ they are 29.16\% and
37.55\%. The claim that these transitions are enhanced because they ``carry the
twin-prime signature'' is therefore withdrawn. The fact that $9 \to 1$ exceeds $1 \to 3$
is a matter of competition within a row: from the digit~1 the competitor $1 \to 7$ has
minimum gap~6 and is boosted by the $p = 3$ factor, while from~9 the competitor
$9 \to 3$ has minimum gap~4. In the model of Section~\ref{sec:model}, removing the
$p = 3$ factor reverses the order of the two entries (Table~\ref{tab:mingaps}).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The diagonal suppression is a gap-length effect.} Every same-digit gap
is a multiple of~10, so it receives the extra $p = 5$ factor $4/3$; the singular series
\emph{favours} the diagonal. Same-digit transitions are suppressed all the same,
because they need a gap of at least~10, and it is less likely that no prime occurs in a
long interval. In the Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan conjecture this is the negative
first-order term $c_1(q;a,a)\log\log x/\log x$ (Section~\ref{sec:findings}). The
suppression survives when all ternary information is removed: with the $p=3$ factor
set to~1, the model still gives diagonal entries between 19.5\% and 21.0\%.
The same holds modulo~3, where the singular series favours the diagonal even more
strongly: same-class gaps are multiples of~6 and receive the \emph{maximal} $p = 3$
factor, yet the frequency of a repeated class
modulo~3 is $44.67\%$ for the first $10^8$ primes and $45.15\%$ for the first $10^9$,
below the uniform~50\%.

\subsection{A Crude Hardy--Littlewood Model}
\label{sec:model}

To compare the three choices of $p = 3$ factor directly, consider the model
\[
  P(d_1 \to d_2) \;\propto \sum_{\substack{h > 0 \\ h \equiv d_2 - d_1 \ (10)}}
  \mathfrak{S}'(h)\, e^{-h/\log x},
\]
normalised over each row, where $\mathfrak{S}'(h)$ is the product of the local factors
of $\mathfrak{S}(h)$ over primes $p \nmid 10$ (the factors at 2 and~5 are fixed by
conditioning on residues modulo~10), and $e^{-h/\log x}$ is a Cram\'er-type
probability that no prime lies in between. We take $x = 2.04 \times 10^9$, the size of
the $10^8$-th prime, truncate the product at $p < 20{,}000$ and the sum at
$h < 40\log x$. This is not the Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan conjecture, only a simple
model built from the same ingredients; the code is \texttt{model.py} in the audit
material.

The results are in Table~\ref{tab:mingaps}. With the correct $p = 3$ factor the model
reproduces the observed matrix to an RMS error of $0.85$ percentage points. It is crude:
its largest error is $1.7$ points (for $1 \to 7$, $28.31\%$ against $30.02\%$), and it
places $1 \to 7$ below $1 \to 3$, whereas the data place it slightly above. With no
$p = 3$ factor the RMS error rises to $2.36$ points. With the factor claimed in the first
version it is $5.94$ points: that factor predicts $1 \to 7 = 19.45\%$ against an observed
$30.02\%$, and it raises the diagonal entries to between $21.39\%$ and $24.70\%$
(observed: $17.76\%$ to $18.49\%$), largely removing the diagonal suppression that the
first version set out to explain.

\begin{remark}
The model and the Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan conjecture both rest on the
Hardy--Littlewood conjecture, and neither is a proof. What the comparison shows is that
the prime~3 matters, through its ordinary local factor $3/2$ or $3/4$, and that the
mechanism proposed in the first version (a vanishing factor and ``redistribution'') is
contradicted by the data. No further ``mechanistic layer'' beneath the singular series is
needed to account for the matrix, and the first version's description of the singular
series as ``the quantitative expression of ternary constitutional structure'' is
withdrawn: the factor at~3 is one local factor among the factors at all primes.
\end{remark}

% ============================================================
\section{The Snail's Pace: The Rate of Decay}
\label{sec:snail}
% ============================================================

The bias fades as the primes grow, but slowly. The phrase ``at a snail's pace'' is
Quanta Magazine's~\cite{Kla16}, not Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan's. Their conjecture
gives the rate explicitly: the leading correction is $c_1(q;a,b)\log\log x/\log x$
(Section~\ref{sec:findings}), which decays even more slowly than $1/\log x$.

\begin{erratumbox}
\noindent The first version identified the snail's pace with the Siegel--Walfi{}sz rate
of equidistribution of primes modulo~3, $|\pi_1(N) - \pi_2(N)| = O(N
\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N}))$, and stated that the digit bias diminishes ``at exactly the rate''
of that bound. The identification, the corresponding proposition, and the figure that
illustrated it are withdrawn.
\end{erratumbox}

The identification fails for four reasons.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
  \item The bias concerns \emph{pairs} of consecutive primes. It is present even though
    single primes are very well distributed among the residue classes, so a bound for
    $|\pi_1(N) - \pi_2(N)|$ cannot be its rate. In any case the last digit carries no
    information about the class modulo~3 (Remark~\ref{rem:crt}).
  \item The bound $N\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N})$ is an upper bound for the error, coming from the
    classical zero-free region for the fixed modulus~3; the Siegel--Walfi{}sz theorem
    \cite{Wal36} makes such a bound uniform for moduli $q \leq (\log N)^A$. It is not the
    true size of the error, and ``at exactly the rate'' of an $O$-bound has no meaning.
  \item The true size is much smaller. The recomputed differences
    $\pi(x;3,2) - \pi(x;3,1)$ are $190$, $420$, $2107$ and $6264$ at
    $x = 10^7, 10^8, 10^9, 10^{10}$, while $\sqrt{x}/\log x \approx 196$, $543$, $1526$,
    $4343$. This is the size of Chebyshev's bias: under the generalised Riemann
    hypothesis and a linear-independence hypothesis,
    $(\log x/\sqrt{x})(\pi(x;3,2) - \pi(x;3,1))$ has a limiting logarithmic
    distribution~\cite{RS94}. The relative imbalance
    $(\pi(x;3,2) - \pi(x;3,1))/\pi(x)$ is $4.1 \times 10^{-5}$ at $x = 10^9$, whereas the
    diagonal entries of Table~\ref{tab:matrix} for the first $10^8$ primes lie on average
    $6.9$ percentage points below~25\%.
  \item The two quantities do not decay together. From $x = 10^7$ to $x = 10^{10}$ the
    relative mod-3 imbalance falls by a factor of about $21$, from $2.9 \times 10^{-4}$ to
    $1.4 \times 10^{-5}$. Over a comparable range (the first $10^6$ primes, up to
    $1.5 \times 10^7$, to the first $10^9$ primes, up to $2.3 \times 10^{10}$) the mean
    diagonal deficit of the base-10 matrix falls only from $8.50$ to $6.28$ percentage
    points, a factor of $1.35$.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, $\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N})$ decays \emph{faster} than any fixed power of
$1/\log N$, and hence faster than $\log\log N/\log N$; a quantity that decays this fast
cannot be the slow decay that Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan derived. The first
version's Figure~3,
which asserted the opposite and plotted a hypothetical curve as ``confirmation'', has been
removed.

\section{Other Moduli, Parity, and the Programme}
\label{sec:validation}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Other Moduli}
\label{sec:bases}

\begin{erratumbox}
\noindent The first version argued that the bias is base-independent because ``the
mechanism operates in mod-3 arithmetic'': in every base the prime last digits map to
ternary classes, and the ternary class of the minimum gap determines the transition
structure. That argument, and the figure that illustrated it for bases 6, 10 and~16, are
withdrawn.
\end{erratumbox}

The argument fails on its own terms. When $\gcd(b, 3) = 1$, as for $b = 10$ and
$b = 16$, the last digit in base~$b$ carries no information about the class modulo~3
(Remark~\ref{rem:crt}); for instance, primes ending in~3 or~9 in base~10 are not
divisible by~3, and the figure's count of ``Forbidden-class prime terminal digits''
contradicted Definition~\ref{def:constraint}. When $3 \mid b$, as for $b = 6$, the last
digit of a prime $p > 3$ is determined by $p \bmod 6$, that is, by $p \bmod 3$; the
same-digit gaps are then multiples of~6 and receive the \emph{maximal} $p = 3$ factor,
yet the diagonal is suppressed (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

The correct statement is the one of Lemke Oliver and Soundararajan: their conjecture is
formulated for every modulus~$q$, and its first-order term depends only on $\varphi(q)$
and on whether $a \equiv b$. The data fit this. Modulo~4, where there is no ternary digit
structure at all, the frequency of a repeated class is $45.04\%$ for the first $10^8$
primes and $45.48\%$ for the first $10^9$, close to the values $44.67\%$ and $45.15\%$
modulo~3; both moduli have $\varphi(q) = 2$.

\subsection{The Parity Barrier}

Selberg's parity problem~\cite{Sel49} is the observation that classical sieve methods
cannot distinguish integers with an even number of prime factors from integers with an
odd number; in other words, they cannot detect the parity of $\Omega(n)$, the sign of
the Liouville function. It has nothing to do with working ``modulo~2''. The first version
described it as a statement about ``sieve methods operating modulo~2'' and cited a
different paper of Selberg's, on the elementary proof of the prime number theorem for
arithmetic progressions; both are corrected here.

For bounded gaps between primes the history is as follows. Zhang~\cite{Zha14} proved
that $\liminf (p_{n+1} - p_n) \leq 7 \times 10^7$. Maynard~\cite{May15} obtained~600,
and~12 under the Elliott--Halberstam conjecture. The Polymath8b
project~\cite{Pol14} obtained~246 unconditionally, using the Bombieri--Vinogradov level of
distribution $1/2$~\cite{Bom65, Vin65}, and~6 under the generalised Elliott--Halberstam
conjecture. The value 246 is therefore set by the level of distribution, not by the
parity problem. It is the parity problem that prevents these sieve methods from going
below~6 even under the generalised Elliott--Halberstam conjecture, and so from reaching
gap~2.

The first version also gave a table of ``ternary signatures'' of gaps and claimed that
gap~2 is the only even gap avoiding the Forbidden class, so that a ternary method could
isolate twin primes where sieves fail. That claim is withdrawn. The table silently fixed
$p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$; for $p \equiv 1$, the pair $(p, p+4)$ avoids the Forbidden class,
as does $(p, p+8)$ for $p \equiv 2$. The correct statement is
Remark~\ref{rem:gaps-mod3}: each gap not divisible by~3 allows exactly one class of
$p \bmod 3$, and each gap divisible by~3 allows both. Table~\ref{tab:gaps} lists the
consequences for the first six even gaps.

In particular cousin primes $(p, p+4)$ are as common as twin primes, as
$\mathfrak{S}(4) = \mathfrak{S}(2)$ predicts, and gap~6 is the most common gap. The
condition $p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$ for twin primes is a local condition of exactly the kind
that every sieve, and the singular series, already take into account. It does not bear on
the parity problem.

\subsection{Relation to the Constitutional Forcing Programme}
\label{sec:relation}

This paper is p08 of the Constitutional Forcing programme~\cite{Wakil26}, which
consists of nine papers, p01--p09. The first version described the programme as having
established a level of distribution $\theta_W = 5/8$ for primes over cascade moduli
$q = 3^K$. That level is a conjecture (p04~\cite{Wakil26d}). For prime-power moduli the
known results are well below~$5/8$, and a level of $5/8$ for each individual modulus
$3^K$ would go beyond what the generalised Riemann hypothesis gives. The value $5/8$ also appears in Pascadi's
work~\cite{Pas25} on smooth numbers and on primes with triply-well-factorable weights,
for general moduli; that result does not concern moduli~$3^K$, and the agreement of the
two numbers is a coincidence, not evidence.

The digit bias is independent of all of this. Its explanation requires the
Hardy--Littlewood heuristic and nothing from the programme. The first version's claims
that the digit bias and the cascade-moduli level are ``projections of the same
underlying structure'', and that the explanation needs only the elementary mod~3
constraint and the Siegel--Walfi{}sz theorem, are withdrawn. The second claim was in any
case inconsistent with the first version's own remark that the explanation inherits the
assumptions of the Hardy--Littlewood conjecture.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

The Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan bias is easy to check: Soundararajan told
Nature~\cite{Lam16} that everyone they told about it ended up writing a program to check
it for themselves. It was explained, heuristically but quantitatively, in the paper that
announced it~\cite{LOS16}. This paper tested a different explanation, ternary
constitutional structure, against recomputed data for the first $10^8$ and $10^9$
primes. The explanation does not survive:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item The diagonal suppression is a gap-length effect, the negative first-order term
    of the Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan conjecture. It is not a singular-series penalty:
    $\mathfrak{S}(10) \approx 1.76$ exceeds $\mathfrak{S}(2) \approx 1.32$, which is the
    smallest even-gap value, and the same-class gaps modulo~3 receive the maximal
    $p = 3$ factor and are still suppressed.
  \item The slow decay is the $\log\log x/\log x$ term of that conjecture. It is not the
    Siegel--Walfi{}sz rate; the mod-3 imbalance of single primes is of order
    $\sqrt{x}/\log x$, far smaller than the digit bias, and it decays far faster.
  \item The bias is not a base-10 projection of mod-3 structure: last digits carry no
    information about the class mod~3, and the bias modulo~4 is about the same as
    modulo~3.
  \item The rule that the ternary class of the minimum gap governs each transition is
    contradicted by the data (gap~2 against gap~8, gap~4 against gap~10), and the
    $p = 3$ factor does not vanish for gaps divisible by~3: it is $3/2$, its maximum.
\end{enumerate}

What the prime~3 does contribute is its ordinary local factor in the singular series,
$3/2$ for gaps divisible by~3 and $3/4$ otherwise. This is why~6 is the most common
gap between consecutive primes in the computed range. The factor also boosts the
transitions $1 \to 7$, $3 \to 9$ and $7 \to 3$ (in the model of
Section~\ref{sec:model}, removing it lowers $1 \to 7$ from $28.31\%$ to $25.34\%$), and
in that model it accounts for $9 \to 1$ exceeding $1 \to 3$.
This effect is part of the Lemke Oliver--Soundararajan explanation, not an alternative to
it. The elementary fact that twin primes $p > 3$ satisfy $p \equiv 2 \pmod 3$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:signature}) is true, but it explains none of the bias.

The conclusion of the first version is therefore reversed. The Lemke
Oliver--Soundararajan explanation accounts for the bias, and the ternary-constitutional
explanation, as originally stated, is not supported.
