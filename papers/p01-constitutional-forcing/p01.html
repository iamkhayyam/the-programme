CONSTITUTIONAL FORCING

KHAYYAM WAKIL

Abstract. We introduce and formally deﬁne Constitutional Forcing: a proposed mechanism by which the algebraic structure of a mathematical system would determine a governing constant, leaving no freedom in its value.

A system of depth \(k\) is deﬁned to have exactly \(k\) independent constitutional constraints, operating on a binary conﬁguration space of size \(2^k\), and we postulate that exactly \(k\) conﬁguration classes are eliminated, leaving \(2^k - k\) valid conﬁgurations. The governing constant is then the conﬁguration density \begin {equation*} \thetak = \frac {2^k - k}{2^k}. \end {equation*} This count is a postulate, not a theorem: if a conﬁguration is called invalid whenever some constraint fails, there are \(2^k - 1\) invalid conﬁgurations, not \(k\).

We examine three candidate instances. The Bombieri–Vinogradov level of distribution \(1/2\) is assigned depth \(k = 1\), but the assignment rests on the binary predicate “prime or not prime”, and the same reasoning would assign \(1/2\) to any predicate. The cascade moduli \(q = 3^K\) are proposed as a depth-\(3\) system with conjectured level \(\theta _3 = 5/8\) [16]; this assignment is currently unsupported. Shannon’s theorems [11] give the constants \(H/\log |\mathcal {A}|\) and \(C\), not \(1/2\), and are retained only as a proposed analogy. We state the Constitutional Forcing Conjecture: every system with \(k\) independent constitutional constraints has \(\thetak = (2^k - k)/2^k\) as its governing constant, and every depth \(k \geq 1\) is realised.

This paper is the ﬁrst of a nine-paper programme (p01–p09). The remaining eight papers develop the proposed instances, their consequences and the cross-domain comparisons.

1. What Constitutional Forcing Is

1.1. The problem this paper addresses. Some constants in mathematics invite the question “why this value and not another?” The Bombieri–Vinogradov exponent \(1/2\) is an example. This paper proposes a framework in which such a constant would be read oﬀ from the number of independent structural constraints on the system, and it states the framework precisely enough that each proposed instance can be checked.

We call it Constitutional Forcing. The name is deliberate. A constitutional constraint is one that is not imposed from outside the system but is inherent to its constitution—to what the system fundamentally is. Constitutional Forcing, if it occurs, is the situation in which a system’s own constitution leaves no room for any governing constant other than the one it has.

1.2. Status of the proposal. The two arithmetic candidates examined in Section 3 rest, so far, on numerical coincidences: the Bombieri–Vinogradov exponent \(1/2\) equals \(\theta _1\), and the exponent \(5/8\) that Pascadi [9] obtained for primes with triply-well-factorable weights to general moduli equals \(\theta _3\). A coincidence of this kind is not evidence. Section 3 checks each candidate against the deﬁnition and records that none currently satisﬁes it in full.

The purpose of this paper is therefore to ﬁx the deﬁnitions on which the eight companion papers rely, and to state precisely what would have to be proved for a system to count as an instance.

1.3. Relationship to the Shannon–Wakil Eﬀect. In the programme’s terminology, Constitutional Forcing is the proposed mechanism and the Shannon–Wakil Eﬀect [18] the proposed observable: the reduction of an exponential conﬁguration space to its valid part. The intended relationship is: \[ \text {Constitutional Forcing} \;\xrightarrow {\text {acts on}}\; \text {exponential space} \;\xrightarrow {\text {produces}}\; \text {Shannon--Wakil Effect}. \] The present paper supplies the deﬁnitions on which [18] relies.

2. The Deﬁnitions

2.1. Constitutional constraints.

Deﬁnition 2.1 (Constitutional constraint). Let \(S\) be a mathematical system with a binary conﬁguration space at each operational level. A constraint \(\mathcal {C}_i\) at level \(i\) is constitutional if it satisﬁes all three of the following:

(C1)
Intrinsic. \(\mathcal {C}_i\) is forced by the irreducible algebraic structure of \(S\)—it is not a free parameter, an external imposition, or a modelling choice.
(C2)
Independent. \(\mathcal {C}_i\) is structurally independent of constraints at other levels: it cannot be derived from, or reduced to, a combination of constraints at levels \(j \neq i\).
(C3)
Binary-eliminating. \(\mathcal {C}_i\) eliminates exactly one conﬁguration class at level \(i\). It does not eliminate more (over-constrained) or fewer (under-constrained). How the eliminated classes are counted in the full conﬁguration space is ﬁxed by Postulate 2.6 below.

Remark 2.2. Condition (C1) is the most important and the hardest to fake. The test is counterfactual: could one choose a diﬀerent value without changing the algebraic structure of the system? If yes, the constraint is not constitutional. If no—if changing the constraint requires changing what the system fundamentally is—then it is.

Remark 2.3. Condition (C2) is what distinguishes Constitutional Forcing from ordinary constraint satisfaction. A system with \(k\) coupled constraints (each derivable from a single underlying constraint) has eﬀective depth \(1\), not depth \(k\). An example is twin-prime admissibility modulo powers of \(3\): for \(p > 3\), the pair \((p, p+2)\) avoids divisibility by \(3\) if and only if \(p \equiv 2 \pmod 3\), and every lift of this class to modulus \(9\) or \(27\) again avoids it, so the “conditions” modulo \(9\) and \(27\) are implied by the condition modulo \(3\) (Section 3.2). Note also that \(\theta _1 = \theta _2 = 1/2\) (checked in the accompanying Lean ﬁles), so the value \(1/2\) alone cannot distinguish depth \(1\) from depth \(2\).

2.2. Constitutional Forcing.

Deﬁnition 2.4 (Constitutional Forcing). A system \(S\) exhibits Constitutional Forcing of depth \(k\) if:

(CF1)
Conﬁguration space. \(S\) has a binary conﬁguration space \(\Ck = \{0,1\}^k\) of size \(2^k\) at depth \(k\). Each conﬁguration \(c = (c_1, \ldots , c_k)\) records whether the constraint at level \(i\) is satisﬁed (\(c_i = 1\)) or violated (\(c_i = 0\)).
(CF2)
\(k\) independent constitutional constraints. \(S\) has exactly \(k\) constitutional constraints \(\mathcal {C}_1, \ldots , \mathcal {C}_k\) in the sense of Deﬁnition 2.1, one per level.
(CF3)
Counting postulate. Exactly \(k\) conﬁgurations of \(\Ck \) are invalid, one attributed to each \(\mathcal {C}_i\) (Postulate 2.6), so that the valid conﬁgurations number \(2^k - k\) and the governing constant is the conﬁguration density \begin {equation} \label {eq:theta} \thetak = \frac {2^k - k}{2^k}. \end {equation}
(CF4)
Uniqueness. The governing constant of \(S\) (for example, its level of distribution) equals \(\thetak \), and it cannot be changed without either removing a constitutional constraint (reducing depth) or adding one (increasing depth).

Remark 2.5. In this revision the formula \((2^k - k)/2^k\) is part of the deﬁnition, through the counting postulate in (CF3); it is not derived. Condition (CF4) carries the substantive content: for a given system it asserts that the system’s governing constant equals \(\thetak \) and is rigid. Verifying (CF4) for a particular system is a mathematical claim about that system and needs its own proof.

2.3. The counting postulate. The February version stated the following as a theorem (the “Counting Theorem”) and gave a proof by induction. The proof is invalid (Remark 2.7), and the statement is now a postulate.

Postulate 2.6 (Counting postulate). In a system of depth \(k\), exactly \(k\) conﬁguration classes in \(\Ck = \{0,1\}^k\) are eliminated, one by each constitutional constraint.

Granting Postulate 2.6, the valid conﬁgurations number \(2^k - k\) out of \(2^k\), and the conﬁguration density is \(\thetak = (2^k - k)/2^k\), as in (1).

Remark 2.7 (Why this is not a theorem). Under (CF1), \(c_i = 1\) exactly when constraint \(i\) holds. If a conﬁguration is called invalid whenever some constraint fails, then a single failing constraint already invalidates the \(2^{k-1}\) conﬁgurations with \(c_i = 0\); in total \(2^k - 1\) conﬁgurations are invalid and exactly one, \((1, \ldots , 1)\), is valid, for every \(k\) (for \(k = 3\): seven invalid, not three). The revised Lean ﬁle P05 proves this count as invalid_count. The withdrawn induction overlooked that each of the \(k - 1\) conﬁgurations invalid at depth \(k - 1\) splits into two at depth \(k\). The count \(k\) is therefore an additional assumption about which conﬁguration classes are counted as eliminated, not a consequence of (CF1) and (CF2).

3. Three Candidate Instances

We examine three candidate instances against Deﬁnition 2.4. None of them currently satisﬁes the deﬁnition in full; for each we record what holds and what does not.

3.1. Prime arithmetic: Bombieri–Vinogradov (\(k = 1\)). The Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem [3, 12] states that for every \(A > 0\) there is \(B = B(A)\) such that \[ \sum _{q \leq x^{1/2}(\log x)^{-B}} \; \max _{(a,q) = 1} \Bigl | \psi (x;q,a) - \frac {x}{\varphi (q)} \Bigr | \ll _A \frac {x}{(\log x)^A}. \] It is a statement on average over the moduli, not one for each \(q \leq x^{1/2}\). The exponent \(1/2\) is its level of distribution.

Proposal 3.1 (Bombieri–Vinogradov as a depth-\(1\) system). It is proposed that the Bombieri–Vinogradov level of distribution \(1/2\) is an instance of Constitutional Forcing at depth \(k = 1\).

Check against Deﬁnition 2.4. (CF1): Conﬁguration space \(\{0,1\}^1 = \{0,1\}\), size \(2\).

(CF2): The proposed constraint is primality. A positive integer either is prime (\(c_1 = 1\)) or is not (\(c_1 = 0\)); the constraint is intrinsic, and there is only one. But the same reasoning applies verbatim to any predicate on the integers (being squarefree, being a sum of two squares, …), and would assign each of them depth \(1\) and constant \(1/2\). As applied here, (CF2) therefore contains nothing speciﬁc to the primes or to the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem.

(CF3): Under Postulate 2.6, one conﬁguration is invalid and one valid, so \(\theta _1 = 1/2\).

(CF4): Not established, and the justiﬁcation given in the February version was incorrect. The Generalised Riemann Hypothesis does not raise the level beyond \(1/2\): for a single modulus it gives the expected asymptotic only for \(q \leq x^{1/2-\varepsilon }\), and a level beyond \(1/2\) for general moduli is the content of the separate Elliott–Halberstam conjecture. Levels beyond \(1/2\) are known unconditionally in restricted settings: \(1/2 + 1/584\) (Zhang [13]) and \(1/2 + 7/300\) (Polymath [10]) for smooth squarefree moduli; and, for a ﬁxed residue class and suitably well-factorable weights, \(4/7\) (Bombieri–Friedlander–Iwaniec [4]), \(3/5\) (Maynard [8]), \(66/107\) (Lichtman [7]) and \(5/8\) (Pascadi [9]). None of \(4/7\), \(3/5\) or \(66/107\) has the form \((2^k - k)/2^k\), so the claim of the February version that any improvement on \(1/2\) requires an increase of depth is not supported. Pascadi’s theorem concerns general moduli; it does not restrict to cascade moduli and does not “increase the depth”.

3.2. Prime arithmetic: cascade moduli (proposed \(k = 3\)). For cascade moduli \(q = 3^K\), the companion paper [16] conjectures a level of distribution \(\theta _W = 5/8\). The February version presented this as an established depth-\(3\) instance. Both the arithmetic statement and the depth assignment are, at present, unsupported.

Proposal 3.2 (Cascade moduli as a depth-\(3\) system). It is proposed that primes have level of distribution \(\theta _3 = 5/8\) to the moduli \(q = 3^K\), and that this is an instance of Constitutional Forcing at depth \(k = 3\). Both parts are conjectural.

Check against Deﬁnition 2.4. (CF1): Conﬁguration space \(\{0,1\}^3\), size \(8\).

(CF2): The February version proposed three constraints on a twin prime pair \((p, p+2)\) with \(p > 3\), at the moduli \(3\), \(9\) and \(27\). Only the ﬁrst is a constraint. Modulo \(3\), \(p \equiv 2\) is the only class for which neither \(p\) nor \(p + 2\) is divisible by \(3\). Modulo \(9\) and \(27\) nothing further is excluded: each of the \(3\) (respectively \(9\)) lifts of \(2 \bmod 3\) keeps both \(p\) and \(p + 2\) prime to \(3\). The conditions modulo \(9\) and \(27\) are implied by the condition modulo \(3\), so (C2) fails and, by the paper’s own criterion, the depth is \(1\). Nor does \(\Z [\omega ]\) supply three levels: since \((3) = (1-\omega )^2\), the \((1-\omega )\)-adic ﬁltration of \(\Z [\omega ]/(3^K)\) has \(2K\) steps, each with quotient \(\mathbb {F}_3\). The “eight conﬁguration classes” were never deﬁned.

(CF3): With one constraint, Postulate 2.6 gives \(\theta _1 = 1/2\). The value \(5/8\) would require three independent constraints, and none has been exhibited beyond the ﬁrst.

(CF4): Not established. Twin-prime admissibility and the level of distribution of primes to the moduli \(3^K\) are, moreover, diﬀerent objects. The arithmetic statement itself is open in its meaningful form:

  • The summed statement \[ \sum _{3^K \leq x^{\theta }} \max _{(a,3) = 1} \Bigl | \psi (x;3^K,a) - \frac {x}{\varphi (3^K)} \Bigr | \ll \frac {x}{(\log x)^A} \] holds for every ﬁxed \(\theta < 1\): for \(3^K > (\log x)^{A+1}\) the trivial bound \(\ll x \log x / 3^K\) sums geometrically, and the Siegel–Walﬁsz theorem covers the \(O(\log \log x)\) remaining moduli. In this form it carries no information about \(5/8\).

  • The per-modulus version (the expected asymptotic for \(\psi (x;3^K,a)\) for each \(3^K \leq x^{5/8-\varepsilon }\)) would go beyond what GRH gives (\(x^{1/2}\)). For prime-power moduli the best known ranges are \(3/8\) (Barban–Linnik–Tshudakov [2]), \(2/5\) (Gallagher [5]) and \(5/12\) (Huxley), with lower bounds of the correct order up to \(x^{0.4736}\) (Banks–Shparlinski); see [1] for an account.

The “uniqueness of \(5/8\)” cited in the February version (an Eisenstein “ceiling \(2/3\)” combined with cyclic-group cancellation) is withdrawn. Pascadi’s exponent \(5/8\) [9] concerns smooth numbers, and primes with triply-well-factorable weights, in progressions to general moduli; its numerical agreement with \(\theta _3\) is a coincidence, not evidence.

3.3. Information theory: Shannon’s theorems (a proposed analogy). The February version presented Shannon’s asymptotic equipartition property (AEP) and channel coding theorem [11] as depth-\(1\) instances. They do not satisfy Deﬁnition 2.4, because their governing constants are not \(\theta _1 = 1/2\).

  • AEP. For a memoryless source with alphabet \(\mathcal {A}\) and entropy \(H\) (logarithms to base \(2\)), the typical set has about \(2^{nH}\) of the \(|\mathcal {A}|^n\) sequences of length \(n\). The natural constant is the exponent ratio \(H/\log |\mathcal {A}|\), which takes every value in \([0,1]\): it is \(0.469\) for a Bernoulli\((0.1)\) source and \(0.811\) for a Bernoulli\((0.25)\) source. The typical set’s share of \(\mathcal {A}^n\) is about \(2^{-n(\log |\mathcal {A}| - H)}\), which tends to \(0\) unless the source is uniform.

  • Channel coding. The capacity is \(C = \max _{p(x)} I(X;Y)\). For a binary symmetric channel with crossover probability \(\rho \) it is \(1 - h(\rho )\), where \(h\) is the binary entropy function; again this takes every value in \([0,1]\).

What the two theorems share with Deﬁnition 2.4 is a binary split (typical or atypical; rate achievable or not). As with primality in Section 3.1, a binary split by itself would assign depth \(1\) to any predicate. We therefore retain the comparison only as a proposed analogy: in both settings a condition intrinsic to the system singles out part of an exponential space, and a constant ﬁxed by the system governs its size. Whether the analogy can be sharpened into an instance of Deﬁnition 2.4 is open; as it stands, the constants \(H/\log |\mathcal {A}|\) and \(C\) do not equal \(\theta _1\).

4. Non-Instances and Falsiﬁability

A framework that cannot be wrong is not mathematics. For Constitutional Forcing to make predictions, the depth \(k\) of a system—its constraints, with (C2) checked—must be ﬁxed before its governing constant is compared with \(\thetak \). Constitutional Forcing at depth \(k\) requires genuinely independent constraints, in the sense of (C2), not merely the appearance of independence; Section 3.2 gives an example where apparent independence fails. The February version did not meet this standard, and its proposed non-instance did not test the framework.

Remark 4.1 (Sophie Germain pairs). The February version treated Sophie Germain pairs \((p, 2p+1)\) as a “non-instance” of depth \(2\) with eﬀective depth \(1\), on the ground that “\(2p+1\) is odd” follows from “\(p\) is odd”. That is no constraint at all: \(2p + 1\) is odd for every integer \(p\). The genuine local coupling is modulo \(3\): for \(p > 3\), the conditions \(3 \nmid p\) and \(3 \nmid 2p+1\) together force \(p \equiv 2 \pmod 3\), the same single condition as for twin primes. Since \(\theta _1 = \theta _2 = 1/2\), the example cannot discriminate between depths \(1\) and \(2\) and is not a falsiﬁcation test. Moreover, a “level of distribution of Sophie Germain pairs” is not a deﬁned notion, and no ﬁnite computation can conﬁrm an asymptotic level, so the claimed computational conﬁrmation is withdrawn. The accompanying Lean ﬁles check \(\theta _1 = \theta _2\) and the mod-\(3\) fact.

5. The Constitutional Forcing Conjecture

Conjecture 5.1 (Constitutional Forcing Conjecture). Let \(S\) be a system exhibiting Constitutional Forcing of depth \(k\) in the sense of Deﬁnition 2.4. Then the governing constant of \(S\) is uniquely forced to be \[ \thetak = \frac {2^k - k}{2^k}, \] and this value is achievable: there exist systems exhibiting Constitutional Forcing at every depth \(k \geq 1\).

Remark 5.2. Conjecture 5.1 has two parts. Under Deﬁnition 2.4 the value \(\thetak \) is built into (CF3) through Postulate 2.6, so the uniqueness part holds by deﬁnition and carries no independent content. The substantive content is the achievability part: for each \(k\), a system must be exhibited whose \(k\) constraints are ﬁxed in advance and satisfy (C1)–(C3), and whose governing constant is proved to equal \(\thetak \), as (CF4) requires. No depth is currently realised in this sense: the \(k = 1\) candidates of Section 3 rest on binary splits that would assign \(1/2\) to any predicate, and the \(k = 3\) candidate is unsupported. The February version singled out \(k = 4\), with governing constant \(3/4\), as the ﬁrst open case; in fact every depth, including \(k = 1\), is open in this sense. Table 1 is the programme’s prediction set, and it becomes falsiﬁable only when \(k\) is ﬁxed for a system before its constant is known.

Remark 5.3. The companion paper [18] discusses a version of this conjecture in the terminology of the Shannon–Wakil Eﬀect. The February version of this remark attributed to [18] a category of Shannon–Wakil systems and a functor to \((0,1)\); no such construction appears there, and none is claimed here.

7. What Constitutional Forcing Is Not

7.1. Not, at present, more than an analogy across domains. The February version claimed that Constitutional Forcing is not an analogy between number theory and information theory but a common, causal mechanism that both instantiate. That claim is withdrawn. Section 3.3 shows that the information-theoretic constants are not \(\theta _1\); the comparison is a proposed analogy, and no causal link is claimed.

7.2. Numerology, and how to avoid it. The formula \(\thetak = (2^k-k)/2^k\) is not derived from the deﬁnitions: it is the counting postulate (Postulate 2.6), and the February claim that “the formula is a theorem, not a ﬁt” is withdrawn. Whether the framework is more than numerology therefore depends on whether \(k\) can be ﬁxed for a system independently of the constant to be explained. The risk is concrete: if \(k\) or the size of the conﬁguration space may be chosen after the target value is known, the formula can be made to match many constants, and since \(\theta _1 = \theta _2 = 1/2\) the value \(1/2\) cannot identify the depth at all. A candidate instance should therefore (i) use the conﬁguration space \(\{0,1\}^k\); (ii) ﬁx \(k\) and the constraints, with (C2) checked, before any comparison with a known constant; and (iii) prove (CF4) for the system. None of the candidates in Section 3 meets all three conditions at present, and the Sophie Germain example (Remark 4.1) is not a test.

7.3. Not a claim of isomorphism. We do not claim that prime arithmetic and information theory are isomorphic, or that there is a functor between them as mathematical objects. The February version claimed that both exhibit Constitutional Forcing; in this revision we claim only a proposed analogy (Section 3.3). Two physical systems that both obey Hamiltonian dynamics share a structural description without being isomorphic; here, even the shared description is not yet established.
